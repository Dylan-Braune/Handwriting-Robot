3. Design and implementation
The Cartesian plotter constrains the workspace, pen state and motion required from the soft-
ware. The design therefore starts with hardware, then follows page segmentation, text/writer
classification and handwriting reproduction, before describing their integration.
Numerical substitutions use the configured mechanism and recorded processing of stored hand-
writing, with inputs and scope collected in Appendix B.7. Borrowed methods are referenced
where introduced; project-specific transforms and heuristics are identified as design choices.
These traces show how the calculations operate; physical qualification remains in Results.

3.2 System block diagram
The complete system connects page capture, handwriting interpretation and physical reproduction.
The camera supplies an image to the ODROID N2+, while the gantry receives motion commands
from the same computer. Between these interfaces, line images, recognised strings, writer
probabilities and pen trajectories are kept as different representations. Figure 1 shows the
physical devices, supplies and signal paths; Figure 2 shows the software stages and the files
passed between them. The text below explains how these two views relate to the project’s
functional units and how a page request reaches the gantry.
The hardware diagram distinguishes USB image data, GPIO control and switch feedback, motor
power and mechanical coupling. The two A4988 drivers supply winding current to the X and Y
motors; the ODROID supplies STEP and DIR signals rather than motor power through GPIO.
Four switches provide axis-limit feedback and a fifth reports pen state. The pen motor has a
separate transistor switching path. The Y drive’s cross-shaft is a mechanical connection between
the two belt sides, not an electrical signal. The server coordinates image acquisition, inference
and job execution.

The software diagram separates preparation, page reading and writing into three lanes. In
preparation, labelled lines and known transcripts feed model training and transcript-guided glyph
extraction; model weights, line caches and writer profiles are stored for later use. In page reading,
conditioning and line segmentation precede the reader and stroke-normalised writer classifier,
whose per-line outputs are combined into a page report. In writing, new text and a selected stored
profile feed glyph layout and candidate scoring, then trajectory generation and a G-code file. The
G-code file is the software-to-hardware boundary: the generator expresses geometry and pen
state, while the gantry interpreter maps those commands to motor and pen signals. Training is
identified as off-device; the request-time processing and motion stages are identified with the
ODROID. The feedback from reader and writer scoring to candidate selection represents the
implemented best-of-N search, not an independent measure of physical handwriting quality.
Recognition and reproduction share models, but they do not perform the same operation. Recog-
nition estimates unknown text from an image. Glyph extraction uses text that is already known
to estimate where its characters occur. Reproduction arranges the extracted pen paths into new
text and uses the reader and writer classifier to select among candidate renderings. Keeping
these roles separate is important: a candidate-selection score indicates how a model judges the
generated image, whereas an independently assessed physical page establishes what the complete
machine actually reproduced.
The final design reads complete lines rather than requiring isolated letters at FU 2.2. Character
boundaries are instead recovered with known transcripts during profile construction. This avoids
committing to uncertain cuts before recognising connected handwriting.
The proposal’s functional units remain identifiable in this architecture. Camera acquisition and
request handling implement FU 1; conditioning and line preparation implement FU 2.1–2.2;
the two trained networks implement classification FU 2.3. Offline weight training corresponds
to FU 2.4, while glyph extraction, profile storage and glyph retrieval implement FU 2.5–2.7.
Trajectory conversion and the motion interpreter implement FU 2.8, followed by the axis drives,
pen holder and lift in FU 3.1–3.4. Training and profile preparation produce stored artefacts
before request-time recognition or writing; they are not repeated for each page.
The system-level distinction is also a practical commissioning boundary. G-code describes mo-
tion in millimetres and pen state without requiring the synthesiser to know GPIO pin assignments.
Conversely, the motion interpreter can execute a geometric test file without loading handwriting
models. A photographed page is therefore transformed into recognised text and a writer estimate
through the read path; a separate writing request selects text and a stored writer profile, creates
a trajectory and G-code, and sends that file to the interpreter. Keeping those requests distinct

3.3 Hardware design
The hardware must hold the paper and pen securely, reach the required writing area, and convert
a stored trajectory into repeatable motion. The design therefore combines a Cartesian belt drive
with a separately actuated pen mechanism. The electrical interface is kept simple enough for
direct control from the ODROID, while calibration relates software coordinates to the travel of
the assembled machine.
3.3.1 Plotter mechanics and mechanical construction
The chosen method of text plotting is a Cartesian gantry system with two independent axes of
movement and a pen up/down mechanism, consistent with the Cartesian arrangement illustrated
by Teikari [1, p. 6, Fig. 4]. Complex kinematic equations and joint-dependent position errors are
avoided by using separate x and y movements for reproduction. Linear GT2 timing belts and
pulleys are used to convert rotary motion from stepper motors and axles to the desired Cartesian
movements. Timing belts were chosen for their cost effectiveness and ability to provide relatively
fast travel without a lead-screw transmission.
A Cartesian gantry consists of stationary rails that guide movement in one axis, and a movable
rail that guides the other axis of movement. For this system and the explanations that follow, the
stationary rails guide y-axis movement, while the x-axis rail moves along the y-axis. Figure 3
shows the axes of the gantry.
Figure 3. Cartesian gantry axes and pen up/down mechanism.

The x-axis rail needs to move with stability and consistency, which is why it is supported at both
ends by the two y-axis rails. A single stepper motor was chosen to drive the pulleys on both sides
to ensure synchronised motion and save costs. Two independent stepper motors driving one axis
introduce a synchronisation concern because differences in missed steps or loading could offset
one side relative to the other. The challenge with using one motor to drive two timing pulleys
is extending its shaft to both pulleys without excessive load or twist in the new axle. A rigid
coupler was used to extend the stepper motor shaft with a chromed steel rod.
To reduce strain on the motors and allow smoother movement, linear bearings were fitted around
the chromed rod rails so that the moving parts slide with low resistance. Bearing alignment is
important as well as bearing diameter: an incorrectly aligned bearing can constrain the same rod
in a different direction from the neighbouring bearing, increasing resistance instead of reducing
it. During assembly, bearing fit and printed-part alignment were checked by sliding the parts
along the rods before connecting the belts. The fit was adjusted where the assembled bearings
restricted movement.
The shared shaft couples the two sides mechanically, but does not remove all sources of error.
Unequal belt tension, shaft torsion and a frame that is not square can still change the pen position.
Belt tension must therefore be sufficient to maintain engagement without adding unnecessary
bearing load. The pen holder also needs enough stiffness to prevent lateral deflection as the
direction of travel changes. These effects concern physical accuracy and cannot be inferred from
the nominal step resolution alone.
The printed components locate bearings, support the motors and pulleys, fasten the belts, and
guide the pen mechanism. Their relationship within the completed assembly is identified in
Figure 4. The arrangement fixes the relative positions of the rail, belt and pen, so manufacturing
fit and bracket stiffness affect the same position budget as the drive. The available travel is
established in Section 3.3.4.

3.3.2 Stepper motor selection and drive resolution
A stepper motor turns through fixed angular increments as the winding-current pattern changes.
The windings are the motor’s coils, and each current pattern establishes a magnetic field that
pulls the rotor towards a corresponding position. The A4988 regulates these winding currents
by chopping: it switches the drive to maintain the requested current level. Chopping continues
even while a position is held; advancing the requested current pattern, rather than chopping itself,
produces the next step [2, p. 9].
Two stepper motors are used in the gantry plotter, one for the x and one for the y direction. The
selected motor is the 42BYGH40-01 NEMA 17 bipolar stepper, with a 40 mm body length, a
1.8○full-step angle and a rated current of 1.68 A per phase. Stepper control suits the required
position commands because displacement follows from the number of commanded steps, without

and ay microsteps with ax ≥ay, the x axis is stepped on each iteration. The accumulator is

3.3.3 Pen lift mechanism and drive circuit
The pen has two required states: in contact with the page for a stroke, and clear of the page for
travel. A compact geared motor and cam provide these states without adding a third positioning
axis. The motor turns in one direction and the cam alternates the pen height as it rotates. A
switch indicates the raised state. Compared with a servo, this arrangement uses a simple switched
output and position indication, but it gives no commanded intermediate pen height or force.
Figure 5 shows the two pen positions. The holder and page support are part of the same design
decision: repeatable motor movement is of little value if a flexible holder changes the nib position
or if a stack of paper changes contact pressure. A single restrained sheet on a rigid support
reduces this variation. The mechanism was adjusted experimentally to achieve clearance during
travel without excessive pressure during drawing.
Figure 5. Cam-operated mechanism with the pen lowered and raised.
The software checks the switch before energising the motor. If the desired state is already present,
no pulse is required; an unnecessary rotation could move the pen away from the requested state.
Otherwise the motor runs until the switch indicates the target or the timeout expires. The current
control routine allows a 40 ms overrun after confirmation of the raised state to seat the cam. This
feedback operation differs from the G-code generator’s settling dwell, which is described in
Section 3.6.8.

The installed pen motor is powered from the ODROID’s dedicated 3.3 V supply pin, while the
GPIO controls the transistor base through its resistor. The power pin and signal pin have different
roles; moving the supply connection away from GPIO does not by itself establish that the rail
can supply motor startup current. No flyback diode is fitted. Interrupting an inductive motor
current can therefore impose a voltage transient on the switching stage [5, section “Protection
and Wiring Techniques” ]. A diode across the motor, reverse-biased during normal operation,
would provide a recirculation path; its current and voltage ratings must be selected from the
motor and supply conditions. This is a limitation of the installed circuit, not a protection feature
already implemented.

3.3.4 End stops, homing and dimensional calibration
Four switches define the minimum and maximum travel of the two axes. Each is connected as
an active-high input with a 10 kΩ pull-down, as shown in Figure 7. The pull-down defines the
input while the contact is open. With the contact closed, its current is 3.3/10000=0.33 mA and
dissipation is approximately 1.1 mW. The resistor is between input and ground, so it does not
limit fault current if the GPIO is incorrectly configured as an output driven low.
The control routine confirms a high reading with two further samples separated by 6 ms. Requir-
ing three consistent readings rejects brief transients, at the cost of waiting approximately 12 ms
when a potential trip is checked. Axis pulses are not continuously generated during this blocking
check, so multiplying the full confirmation interval by the commanded feed would not correctly
describe the software’s movement during debounce. Physical stopping distance also depends on
the mechanics; it requires a measurement rather than a software-delay calculation alone.

3.3.5 Electrical integration and power distribution
The motor-driver supply is operated at 24 V . The recorded supply-current indication is 0.35 A,
with the supply current limit set to 2.11 A. These quantities have different meanings: the first is
a reading at an operating condition, the second limits the available supply current, and neither
specifies the winding-current regulation of an A4988. At the recorded operating condition,
that supply delivers approximately 24(0.35)=8.4 W. This reading does not include a verified
peak-load test or establish total consumption from both supplies.
The ODROID-N2+ header provides 3.3 V digital I/O, and the A4988 logic supply operates from
3 V to 5.5 V [7, “GPIO (40Pin header)” and “External I/O” sections, unpaginated][2, PDF p. 5,
“Logic Supply V oltage Range”]. With the drivers’ logic supply connected to 3.3 V , the A4988
minimum high input is 0.7(3.3)=2.31 V [2, PDF p. 4, Table 2]. A nominal 3.3 V ODROID
output therefore has 3.3−2.31=0.99 V of nominal high-level margin. This is a compatibility
calculation from specified levels, not a measurement at the installed driver pins. Hardkernel
recommends a 12 V , 2 A adapter for the board, but the actual adapter fitted to this system has not
been identified; the value shown in the supplied sketch is therefore not treated as a measured
supply specification [7, “Power” section, unpaginated].

3.3.7 Hardware verification and practical limitations
The purpose of hardware verification is to establish that a coordinate command produces the

included in the draft is useful for this purpose (Figure 10). Its visual agreement supports correct
interpretation of the commanded shape, but does not by itself establish the proposal’s absolute
accuracy or repeatability specification.
The design’s principal strength is the direct relationship between orthogonal coordinates and
motor displacement, combined with a mechanically shared drive on the two sides of the moving
rail. Its principal uncertainties lie in physical compliance and open-loop execution. A missed

3.4 Page conditioning and line segmentation
The recogniser requires an image containing one line of text. A photograph, however, may
contain uneven illumination, a rotated page, printed rules, page edges and strokes extending
between neighbouring lines. The purpose of this stage is to remove those disturbances while
preserving the shape and relative spacing of the handwriting. The implemented sequence in
segment.py works mainly on a half-resolution image, then returns to the full-resolution image
to build the line crops.
3.4.1 Input conditions and processing sequence
The working image is a two-dimensional array of grey values. Where a colour array reaches the
deskew routine, its channels are averaged to obtain brightness. Reducing the analysis image by
averaging each 2×2block reduces its pixel count by a factor of four. Averaging is preferred to
selecting every second pixel because a thin stroke then contributes to the reduced image even if
it falls between sampling positions. A further reduced mask is sufficient for the angle search,
where only the arrangement of whole lines is needed.

3.4.3 Page orientation and skew correction
Orientation is treated at two scales. A quarter turn changes which axis contains the line gaps; a
small skew changes how sharply ink is concentrated along rows. For the quarter-turn decision,
the code compares the fraction of nearly empty rows in the current image and its 90○rotation. A
turn is accepted only when the rotated score exceeds the original by a factor of 1.35. This margin
avoids rotating a page merely because two weak scores differ slightly. Sparse or unusually
arranged text can still make the row-gap evidence ambiguous.

3.4.4 Line detection and separation
The line pitch P provides the main scale reference for later decisions. A smoothed row profile
is formed from the central writing region; candidate peaks must exceed 6% of its maximum.
The preferred estimate is the median distance between peaks, subject to a lower bound based
on component height. This check prevents the ascender, body and descender bands of a single
large line from being treated as separate line spacings. Autocorrelation and a component-height
estimate provide fallbacks when direct gaps are unsuitable.
Line centres are selected in order of peak strength, with a minimum separation of 0.6P. This
suppresses several peaks within the same line. The code also supports merging peaks separated
by a shallow valley, but that additional rule is not automatically active for every photographed
page; its default threshold disables merging. The distinction matters because a rule that improves
rendered text need not improve the shadows and artefacts of a real photograph.

3.4.5 Ink ownership and classifier crop preparation
Seams initially divide the image into bands, but a pixel-by-pixel cut can separate a dot from its
stem or clip a curved descender. Connected-component ownership therefore refines the band
assignment. Components are found with row runs and union operations, following connected-
component labelling principles [13, pp. 476–479]. The implementation includes diagonal contact,
giving eight-connected components. Large pieces can still contain writing from more than one
line, so component labelling alone is not a complete line assignment.
Algorithm 3 summarises the correction order. For a component spanning several bands, a
band holding at least 15% of its in-band ink is substantial. The uppermost substantial band
supplies the reference owner for small-piece corrections; otherwise the largest overlap is used.
Large components are not indiscriminately moved into that band: their pixels retain seam-band
assignment except where the small-piece and narrow-tail rules apply. This distinction preserves
a word’s main body while recovering a protruding part.

3.5 Neural network design, training and classification
The classification stage has two outputs: the text in a line and the identity of its writer. Both
depend on stroke features, but text recognition must preserve their horizontal order while writer
identification must combine evidence across the line. Two networks therefore use the same
convolutional architecture with different heads. The recogniser follows the CNN–BiLSTM
approach of Kizilirmak and Yanikoglu [14, pp. 4–5]; the project implements and integrates that
architecture with its own preprocessing, training and handwriting-reproduction pipeline.
3.5.1 Training data, labels and evaluation separation
Public IAM handwriting [15, page to verify] [verify source/page: marti2002] and the photo-
graphed personal contributions provide examples with known text and writer identity. Public
line images supply text-recognition training data, while writer-labelled pages and the personal
samples support writer identification and style extraction. The eleven writers selected for the
reported evaluation and intended demonstration are six database writers (150, 152, 153, 384,
552 and 588) and five personal contributors. A stored profile, a classifier output class and an
evaluated writer are different objects; the checkpoint’s recorded class mapping defines how its
output indices are interpreted.
The data pipeline pairs every crop with its transcript and writer label. The same preparation
functions are used by the first-principles and framework training routes so that a comparison does
not silently change segmentation or label handling. Blank or unusable labelled lines are excluded.
Characters outside the configured alphabet are filtered by the label encoder; they cannot be
recognised as separate output symbols unless the alphabet and output layer are extended.
The page-based writer dataset holds out an IAM page per writer, while the personal data use a
fixed-seed 15% line split. A page split and a line split provide different separation: a held-out
personal line can share camera conditions, page background and writing session with training
lines. It is therefore appropriate to describe those samples as held-out lines, rather than as wholly
unseen capture sessions. Augmented versions of a sample must remain within its assigned
training partition.
Preparing public forms also requires excluding printed prompts and signatures before matching
detected lines to their transcripts. The eleven-writer classifier corpus contains 1186 labelled lines:
517 IAM and 669 photographed lines, split into 1031 training and 155 held-out lines. The final
reader instead uses a joint corpus of Teklia pre-cut IAM lines and the five photographed writers;
its exact training and validation counts are not preserved in the available record.
Evaluation separation concerns three inputs: the writer classifier’s training lines, the reader’s
training lines and the pages used to build glyph profiles. Holding a line out of one classifier does
not exclude it from the other corpus, and constructing a profile from an evaluation page already

3.5.4 Writer identification and stroke normalisation
Writer identification should combine the shape evidence distributed along a line. It therefore
averages the 160 column features rather than passing them through a sequence decoder. A
128-element feature vector is mapped through a 96-unit hidden layer, ReLU and a final K-class
output. Dropout with probability 0.2 is active in the hidden head during training and omitted
during inference [ 20, pp. 1931, 1934]. Here K is the number of writers represented by that
checkpoint.
The initial classifier operated on handwriting as photographed and performed well on those
samples. Ink width can itself carry writer-identification information: Brink et al. explicitly use
directional ink-trace width as a feature and relate it to the pen and writing style [ 21, p. 162].
The photographed input also retains darkness and capture variation, which the initial network
was not constrained to ignore. The gantry uses its own pen, so reproducing the selected writer’s
geometry does not reproduce the same ink appearance. A classifier that relies on those cues
would therefore provide an unsuitable basis for judging whether the robot preserved the writer’s
style.
The later classifier uses stroke-normalised input to restrict this source of variation. Ink is
binarised, reduced to a centreline with Zhang–Suen thinning [22, p. 237], then expanded using
morphological dilation [12, p. 533, Def. 1] before the fixed-canvas fit. Equation 22 combines
these established operations with the project-selected width rule.

3.5.5 Training procedure and model selection
The implemented training routes update the convolutional backbone and task-specific head
together. Convolution weights use variance-scaled initialisation; normalisation scales begin at
one and offsets at zero. Training-time augmentation changes line geometry while preserving
its label. The implemented transforms include shear, small rotations, perspective changes and
elastic distortion. These operations are applied only to the training view, while validation retains
a consistent preprocessing path.
The text trainer supports public lines, page-derived lines and a joint source that mixes public and
personal examples. In the joint configuration, a personal-sample fraction controls their represent-
ation rather than letting a much larger public dataset determine the entire batch distribution. The
default fraction is 0.20. This is a sampling choice, not a claim that personal and public samples
are interchangeable. Its value and the training source must accompany any comparison between
checkpoints.

3.5.6 First principles and framework implementations
The first-principles route in train.py evaluates the forward pass, backward pass, loss and
optimiser updates using explicit NumPy operations. NumPy supplies array arithmetic and
matrix operations; it does not supply a convolutional network, automatic differentiation or a
CTC training function in this route. The learned parameters are stored as named arrays. The
inference module reads these arrays and evaluates the forward operations without requiring a
machine-learning framework.
The framework route in trainlibrary.py expresses the corresponding networks using
PyTorch layers and automatic differentiation. Both routes use the project’s data preparation,
parameter naming and named-array export layout. This makes the framework implementation
useful as a numerical reference while allowing either training route to supply weights to the
NumPy inference code.
The selected reader and eleven-writer weights were trained using PyTorch and exported to named
arrays. The first-principles contribution comprises the implemented numerical training operations
and the operational NumPy forward path. Fixed-parameter comparisons verify the numerical
calculations; the provenance of the selected weights remains the framework training route.
Matching the architecture is not sufficient to establish a matched training experiment. The current
numerical CTC function averages unnormalised sequence losses across the batch, whereas the
framework trainer constructs CTC with its default mean reduction, which normalises each

also zeroes impossible-alignment losses. These conventions must be matched for a controlled

3.6 Style extraction and handwriting reproduction
Recognising a line does not provide the motion needed to write it. The reproduction stage must
recover usable pen paths from a writer’s examples, store the properties that distinguish that
writer, and assemble the paths into text that was not necessarily present in the examples. The
chosen representation is a library of normalised glyph trajectories accompanied by line-level
style measurements. Profile extraction and trajectory assembly are implemented in style.py
and synthesise.py, respectively. This representation provides explicit control over scale
and movement, while making the limits of character-based reproduction visible.
3.6.1 Choice of reproduction representation
The available writer samples are a few labelled pages photographed after writing. A glyph library
can reuse these observations without fitting a separate generative network for each writer. Haines
et al. provide a relevant precedent: their standard experiments used approximately four A4 pages
per writer [24, PDF p. 4, Sec. 4]. This is evidence that a small-page collection can support a
glyph-based approach, not a guarantee that four pages cover every character or writing context.
The present library likewise depends on the characters and variants actually observed.
The offline data also influenced the choice. Graves’s recurrent synthesis model learns from
ordered pen positions and end-of-stroke markers [ 18, p. 18]; these are not recorded in this
project’s photographs. A skeleton supplies geometric paths, but cannot recover their true order,
speed or pressure. Using it as measured online training data would introduce an unsupported
assumption.
Learned image generators remain a valid alternative, including methods that need very few
references from a new writer. For example, One-DM uses one style reference at generation,
but its IAM experiment trains with 339 writers and its implementation uses four RTX 3090
GPUs [25, p. 9]. A small reference set must therefore be distinguished from the corpus and
computation needed to train the generator. Developing and training an additional generator from
first principles on the ODROID was outside the chosen computational scope; no training-time
comparison was measured. The glyph approach instead retains explicit trajectories that can be
scaled, joined and converted directly to machine coordinates. Its trade-off is weaker modelling
of connected, context-dependent letterforms, examined at the end of this subsection.

3.6.3 Skeleton tracing and pen path construction
Ink is an area, whereas the gantry needs a path. Zhang–Suen thinning reduces the line mask
to a one-pixel centreline [22, p. 237]. The complete line is thinned before character cells are
separated. Thinning each cut cell independently would introduce an artificial image edge at every
character boundary and could change the recovered centreline there.
The skeleton is treated as a graph: adjacent ink pixels form edges, and endpoints and junctions
determine where paths begin or split. Redundant diagonal edges are suppressed when an
orthogonal route already connects the same local pixels. The default tracer first splits paths at
junctions, prunes short dangling spurs, then greedily rejoins nearby ends with a continuation
angle below 75○. Thus the default is not simply one pen lift per junction. A separate through-

order: the image records final ink, and several stroke sequences can create the same graph.

deviation, following the polyline-simplification principle of Douglas and Peucker [ 26, page
to verify] [verify source/page: douglas1973]. Short spurs and cut ligature tails require care
because a genuine small stroke can resemble a skeleton artefact. Isolated dots may thin to a single

glyph and inflate the profile’s connectedness.

3.6.4 Glyph selection and library quality
The library must retain variation without giving every cut equal credibility. A recogniser can
assign the correct transcript while placing one boundary inside a letter, so alignment is followed
by geometric and consistency checks. Low-confidence lines are filtered relative to the writer’s
own alignment distribution. Per-glyph checks reject implausible width, height, stroke count, pen
length and disagreement between ink width and the allocated character advance.
The width gate allows up to 2.1 x-heights for ordinary characters and 2.9 for wide characters
such as m and w. Further width and height checks use the character’s own distribution, rather than

D.R. Braune Design and implementation
applying one absolute size to every letter. The 40th-percentile width provides a reference less
easily enlarged by a few cuts that contain part of a neighbour. Tightening is retained only when
enough variants survive to form a usable pool. These gates encode plausible glyph geometry;
their exact values are engineering heuristics, not universal typographic laws.
A cross-writer shape prior supplies a second check. Each variant is represented by a smoothed,
normalised 12×12occupancy descriptor. Pooling descriptors of the same character across writers
gives a robust reference shape. Its role is to reject malformed cuts from a writer’s own library,
not to copy another writer’s letter into it. Internal consensus then favours variants close to the
writer’s typical form. The ordinary variant pool is limited to twelve per character; an accepted
denoised prototype can be prepended as an additional candidate.

distance is d(g,c)=1−v Tg rc. Internal consensus instead uses pairwise L1 descriptor distances and
chooses the medoid with the lowest total distance. These compare a pooled character reference
with this writer’s own forms. Dylan’s first storeda variant has recorded prior distance 0.114.

3.6.8 Machine coordinate mapping and G-code generation
The selected trajectory is mapped into the calibrated workspace. Normalised glyph size, line
spacing and machine margins are handled separately: fitting the height of a tall descender should
not silently change the meaning of the ruling or move a line outside the available area. Robust
extent measurements limit the effect of a single unusually tall glyph on the scale of the complete
passage.
The physical x-height is first chosen from the page ruling and a stored x-height-to-pitch ratio
where available. If that ratio is absent, the configured fallback gives 8.4(0.46)=3.864 mm at
the default 8.4 mm ruling. This is the fallback sizing rule, rather than the value passed to every
job: the traced Owen job explicitly requests 4 mm. A probe of the requested text can reduce
the selected value if robust line extent exceeds 1.25 rulings. The extent uses the 98th-percentile
stroke tops and second-percentile bottoms, with exact extremes for fewer than eight strokes. It
limits outliers but does not guarantee every extreme lies inside the nominal band.
Two placement modes are supported. Ruled placement adjusts each line’s measured centre to the
configured ruling independently of the character scale. Free placement preserves the synthesised
pitch after scaling, which can avoid forcing large ascenders and descenders from neighbouring
lines into contact. A line offset selects a later starting position and reduces the remaining page
height. Algorithm 8 summarises the conversion before file generation. The full conversion
procedure is retained as Algorithm 8 in Appendix A.9.

3.6.9 Reproduction verification and design limitations
The reproduction checks follow the representations established above: aligned source ink, stored
glyph paths, assembled trajectories and emitted machine commands. Re-rendering the emitted
G-code can reveal a coordinate inversion, scale change, missing stroke or additional joining
segment before drawing. Comparing that render with the selected trajectory checks conversion;
comparing the physical drawing with the render checks execution. These comparisons localise
errors without substituting for end-to-end qualification.
Appendix B shows the same sentence generated from four stored profiles alongside paths
independently parsed from their emitted G-code. Nine controlled conversion fixtures addition-
ally preserved path vertex counts with no clipping and a maximum coordinate discrepancy of
0.0004 mm, consistent with writing coordinates to three decimal places. Joins and ring closure
were disabled only for those correspondence fixtures so that the vertices could be paired; the
four profile illustrations used the normal configuration. Neither check measures the mechanism’s
positional accuracy.
The representation’s main advantage is that its geometry is explicit and inspectable. A defective
glyph can be traced to a source crop, stored paths can be viewed independently of the machine,
and physical size follows from a defined transformation. The main limitation is the loss of
context when a connected word is divided into reusable characters. Real letterforms change
with their neighbours, whereas a finite glyph pool and join rules approximate that dependence.
Skeletons also omit pressure and true temporal order. These losses explain why a plausible letter
library need not reproduce every connected style equally well.
Possible extensions include context-dependent multi-character units, better alignment, trajectory
acquisition and a verified alternative junction tracer. Each addresses a different loss: multi-
character units preserve local letter interactions, trajectory acquisition restores temporal in-
formation, and improved tracing changes how an existing skeleton becomes strokes. Their
implementation would require corresponding changes to extraction, storage or the source data,
rather than a larger candidate search alone. The retained design therefore prioritises extraction
from the available offline pages and explicit control of the plotted geometry. Recovering natural
pen dynamics would require new trajectory data, while replacing the library with a learned
generator would add a separate training and validation problem. The alternative tracer remains
an implementation option; its presence alone does not establish an improvement over the default.

3.7.2 Embedded execution, interfaces and job control
The ODROID N2+ runs NumPy inference and motion control from stored arrays, without the
training framework. Canvas size, line count and candidate count still determine its processing
load. NumPy supplies array arithmetic; Flask handles requests; image libraries handle file I/O;
OpenCV serves the camera interface; andgpiod exposes GPIO. Framework network operations
belong to the separate training route. The complete source code is supplied separately from this
report for the AMS submission.
During motion, the pulse loop checks an abort event and confirmed end stops. A separate request
thread permits a stop while drawing is active. End-stop trips latch a fault; the recovery routines
attempt switch retraction, pen raising and parking under their required preconditions. The stop
request is a software path, not an independent motor-power disconnect. A pen-state timeout
remains consequential because subsequent travel may mark the sheet.
The resulting job states distinguish generation, refused execution, completed execution and
interruption/failure. Calibration establishes the coordinate reference in software, but missed steps
can invalidate its correspondence with physical position. Neither a saved preview nor emitted
pulse counts prove that the nib followed the intended path.

A.3 Morphology, thinning and path simplification
For binary mask M and neighbourhood K, dilation is the maximum of translated mask values
and erosion is their minimum. Closing applies dilation followed by erosion with the same
footprint [12, pp. 533, 541, Defs. 1, 36]. For a binary mask and symmetric flat footprint, the set
operations correspond to neighbourhood maximum and minimum. The implementation obtains
rectangular neighbourhood counts through prefix sums; binary comparisons then implement

these operations. Connectivity analysis uses horizontal runs and union operations with diagonal
overlap admitted, rather than a library component-labelling call.
The thinning conditions follow Zhang and Suen [ 22, p. 237]. The method inspects the eight
neighbours P2, . . . ,P9 clockwise from north. A removable foreground pixel has two to six
foreground neighbours and exactly one background-to-foreground transition around that ring.
The two alternating subpasses additionally require, respectively,
P2P4P6=0,P 4P6P8=0 (first subpass),
P2P4P8=0,P 2P6P8=0 (second subpass). (38)
During thinning of the actual Owen crop, the first recorded deletion in subpass one was pixel
(y,x)=(12,190) with neighbours (P2, . . . ,P9)=(0,0,1,1,1,1,0,0) . Its neighbour count is four
and its circular transition count is one. Both first-subpass products are zero because P2=0 and
P8=0 , so the pixel is deleted when the subpass mask is applied. The deletion is applied with the
other eligible pixels in that subpass.
Candidates in Equation 38 are deleted together in each subpass, and the pair of subpasses repeats
until unchanged. This reduces thickness while limiting topology changes; it cannot restore ink
already lost during thresholding. The same thinning primitive supports stroke normalisation,
glyph tracing and the diagnostic retracing route.
The default tracer begins at graph nodes whose degree differs from two, follows unused pixel
edges, and then recovers remaining closed loops. Short dangling spurs are pruned, and nearby
chain ends are greedily rejoined when their continuation angle is below 75○. This remains
a geometric estimate rather than a verified natural handwriting order. Polyline simplification
compares interior points with the line through the endpoints, retaining a point and splitting
recursively when the deviation exceeds tolerance. The code adapts the polyline reduction
method [26, page to verify] [verify source/page: douglas1973]. Near-closed paths are first
split at a distant point so that a zero-length endpoint chord does not collapse the loop. The
extraction tolerance is 0.6 source pixels before normalisation; final path smoothing and machine
decimation occur in their own coordinate units.

B.6 Post-design seam-coefficient sensitivity
A new one-at-a-time check examines the existing seam weights. Owen01 and Yeukita01 are
reduced to a maximum dimension of 1600 pixels, then passed through orientation, conditioning
and line-centre estimation. The first, middle and last gaps on each page are selected by index
before inspecting outputs. Eight settings share these six gaps, step limit seven and ink weight
four: midpoint weight varies at step penalty 0.02, then step penalty varies at midpoint weight
0.012. This is post-design sensitivity, not a joint search or recovered historical tuning.
