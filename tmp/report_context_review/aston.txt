1. Literature study
1.1 Problem statement: general perspective
Mobile autonomous robots require state estimates before they can execute control commands.
A common approach equips each robot with on-board sensors: wheel encoders measure wheel
rotation, inertial measurement units (IMUs) measure acceleration and angular velocity, and
optical distance sensors measure range to nearby surfaces. These measurements can be combined
to estimate the robot’s motion and surroundings. Although this approach can work well for
individual robots, fitting every vehicle with sensors adds cost, mass and wiring complexity as the
fleet grows.
An alternative is to use sensors installed in the environment to estimate the states of multiple
robots. This reduces the need for on-board sensing, but makes the fleet dependent on the external
sensing and processing infrastructure. A failure in that infrastructure can affect state estimates
for every robot. Accuracy also depends on factors beyond image resolution and calibration,
including camera placement, visibility and detection performance.
This project investigates centralised state estimation using four sensorless mobile robots on a
1.5×1.5mboard in a competitive capture-the-flag game. An overhead camera is the only source
of spatial measurements used by the control loop. The central processor estimates each robot’s
two-dimensional position (x,y)and heading angle θ from camera images, and separately scans
the board to map static obstacles. No other sensors provide spatial measurements to the controller.
Control decisions are calculated centrally and transmitted to the fleet over a wireless link.
Centralised processing was selected because the overhead camera provides a common view of
the board and the fleet is small and fixed at four robots (N=4 ). The central processor performs
perception, pose estimation, goal assignment and route planning, allowing it to coordinate the
attackers’ goals and routes in one place. A common camera view does not mean perfect state
knowledge: occlusion, image resolution and detection errors can still affect the measurements.
1.2 Problem statement: specific perspective
The approved Project Proposal explicitly prohibits external vision and image-processing libraries
for object detection or classification, and excludes pathfinding libraries entirely. Published
methodologies that rely on proprietary library routines for image rectification, fiducial decoding,
or automated graph generation cannot be imported directly. The literature was therefore analysed
for its underlying principles and mathematical formulations, not its software implementations.
Two key system performance metrics were derived from published theoretical bounds rather than
empirical intuition; these sources therefore underpin the specification:
1. Path-Length Optimality (Requirement 5):The 8% path-length tolerance is derived from
worst-case discretisation bounds on eight-connected grids.
2. Localisation Accuracy (Requirement 2):The 25mm spatial positioning budget was
established relative to reported infrastructure-camera accuracy bounds.

1.3 Overhead vision as the sole sensor
Shim and Cho [ 1] demonstrated full mobile robot localisation using fixed ceiling-mounted
cameras, arguing that replacing expensive onboard sensing with static infrastructure scales
economically across multi-robot fleets. Their vehicle carried no onboard localisation sensors,
receiving state estimates wirelessly from a central server—matching the architectural constraints
of this project. Their physical setup utilised obliquely mounted cameras, requiring homography
matrices fitted from chessboard calibration targets to rectify individual views onto a common
ground plane. Post-rectification, their system achieved a mean spatial position error of 26mm
and a worst-case error of 71mm.
While a 71mm error bound would violate the 25mm threshold mandated by Requirement 2, this
discrepancy stems from optical geometry rather than algorithmic limitations. Shim and Cho
merged wide oblique perspectives over a large floor area, causing individual pixels to subtend
large, non-uniform ground areas where residual homography projection errors dominated.
In contrast, this project positions a single top-down camera 1530mm directly above a compact
1.5×1.5mboard. The optical axis remains perpendicular to the ground plane, rendering scale
approximately uniform across the field of view and reducing perspective correction from a
primary error source to a minor secondary refinement. The resulting ground sampling distance
(derived in Section 3.3) is approximately 0.63mm/pixel. Under this resolution, the 25mm
requirement spans roughly 40 pixels, confirming that the hardware resolution is sufficient. The
millimetric positioning target is thus physically achievable because the geometric degradation
present in ceiling-camera literature was eliminated at the structural design stage.
1.4 Colour markers and pose estimation
Fiducial marker systems represent the standard approach for recovering planar pose from over-
head imagery. Ulrichet al.[ 2] detailed a real-time fiducial pipeline capable of full six-degree-of-
freedom pose estimation, offering robust performance under variable lighting. However, fiducial
pipelines operate by detecting quadrilateral borders, rectifying interior bitgrids via homography,
and decoding binary matrices, a sequence that relies heavily on standard computer-vision librar-
ies, which are prohibited by Section 4 of the Project Proposal. The performance guarantees of
standard fiducial frameworks were therefore unavailable, which reflects a software-specification
constraint rather than a hardware limitation.
To comply with the first-principles mandate, a custom two-dot asymmetric colour marker was
adopted. Gevers and Smeulders [ 3] evaluated colour-space invariance under changing view
direction, surface orientation, and illumination intensity. They demonstrated that raw RGB and
intensity channels are highly sensitive to illumination variations, whereas normalised chromaticity
measures remain invariant to light intensity and matte surface shading. Importantly, chromaticity
metrics remain sensitive to shifts in the spectral composition (colour temperature) of ambient
light.

1.5 Graph representation and optimal path planning
Because the maze layout remains static between board scans and features a uniform wall grid
pitch, the environment is discretised into a planar occupancy grid, transforming route generation
into a graph-based shortest-path problem. Hart, Nilsson, and Raphael [ 4] formulated the A∗
search algorithm and proved the mathematical condition required for heuristic search optimality:
the heuristic function h(n)must beadmissible, meaning it must never overestimate the true
remaining cost to the goal node. Under an admissible heuristic, the route to the first goal node
expanded by A∗is guaranteed to be optimal and of minimum cost. Requirement 5 specifies that
generated paths must not exceed the true shortest path length by more than 8%. To assert this
claim, the underlying search algorithm must be strictly optimal on its representation; hence, the
admissibility of the selected octile distance heuristic is formally proven in Section 3.8.
However, graph optimality does not imply continuous spatial optimality. A path constrained to
an eight-connected grid is restricted to heading increments of 45○, making it longer than a true
continuous planar path. Nash and Koenig [5] analysed this discretisation penalty, proving that
for two-dimensional eight-neighbour square grids with vertex-centred transitions and binary cell
occupancy, an optimal grid path is at most≈1.08times longer than the true continuous Euclidean
shortest path. This worst-case bound (≈8%excess) is asymptotically tight. They further noted
that applying post-search path smoothing (string-pulling) cannot increase path length due to the
triangle inequality, though it preserves original path topology.
Grid discretisation introduces a secondary geometric artefact not captured by the distance bound:
diagonal transitions between grid cells pass directly through the single vertex shared by adjacent

obstacles through an infinitely narrow gap. While valid in matrix index arithmetic, this motion
causes physical collisions for finite-sized robots. This edge case is explicitly resolved by enforcing
corner-clearing motion constraints during neighbour expansion (Section 3.8).
Nash and Koenig’s theoretical bound directly informed Requirement 5. It confirms that the8%

1.6 Coordination and adversarial detection
When multiple autonomous agents plan routes independently within a shared workspace, mutual
spatial interference can lead to system-wide deadlock. ˇCápet al.[ 6] analysed failure modes in
prioritised multi-agent path finding (MAPF), where agents plan sequentially in priority order.
They identified two fundamental conflict structures:
• Type A Conflict:A higher-priority agent reaches its target node and stops, permanently
obstructing the only viable path of a lower-priority agent.
• Type B Conflict:A higher-priority agent traverses through the designated starting position
of a lower-priority agent before that agent has departed.
To resolve these failures, ˇCápet al.proved that requiring each agent to avoid both the space-time
trajectories of higher-priority agents and the static initial positions of lower-priority agents guar-
antees deadlock-free execution, provided the environment satisfies awell-formed infrastructure
condition, which requires that target and start locations do not permanently cap flow capacity.
This formulation yielded two conclusions for this project. First, Type A conflicts represent
a practical risk: during initial system testing, an uncoordinated run resulted in the primary
attacking agent reaching the base at t=109s and stopping, blocking the secondary attacker for
the remaining 190s. The goal-vacating strategy implemented in Section 3.11 directly resolves this
issue by instructing an arrived agent to vacate the target node to an adjacent non-obstructive cell.
Second, the theoretical guarantees in MAPF literature rely strictly on well-formed environments;
a 1.5m maze board containing single-width corridors narrower than two robot diameters violates
well-formedness. Therefore, deadlock-freedom cannot be claimed globally, and corridor head-on
encounters are explicitly defined as unresolvable operational boundaries.
Adversarial defender detection is modelled using visibility geometry. A defender detects an
attacker when the attacker enters a directional field-of-view cone with an unobstructed line-
of-sight. This model is rooted in visibility-based pursuit-evasion theory. Gerkey, Thrun, and
Gordon [7] introduced the φ-searcher model to address non-omnidirectional sensing, defining an
agent equipped with a limited angular field of viewφ and unable to see through opaque obstacles.
This project adapts theφ-searcher model in three ways:
1. Relaxed Pursuer Strategy:Defenders follow deterministic patrol paths rather than com-
puting complete pursuit-evasion strategies; detection is opportunistic.

2. Approach
This section sets out the high-level methodology and architectural strategy behind the engineering
solution. The detailed implementation choices follow in Section 3.1.
2.1 System concept
The core problem is adversarial navigation under complete agent blindness. Two attacking robots
cross a 1.5×1.5mmaze towards an opposing base without being detected by two patrolling
defenders. No vehicle carries onboard sensors, so a single overhead camera is the only source of
spatial information. Control is fully centralised: an off-board processor computes every decision
and transmits it wirelessly to the fleet, which executes open-loop motion between updates.
The architecture was conceived as one unified feedback loop, not a collection of independent
modules. Each incoming camera frame initiates a sequential processing pipeline:
1. Perception:Localises and classifies all four agents, extracting position (x,y)and heading
θ.
2. Planning & Threat Assessment:Computes or revalidates obstacle-free routes for the
attackers while updating defender threat regions.
3. Control & Communication:Generates corrective steering commands and broadcasts
drive signals via a wireless link.
4.State Evaluation:Checks win, capture, and timeout game-ending conditions.
Nothing persists between loop iterations except the static wall map and high-level game state.
A dropped frame costs a single cycle; it cannot desynchronise the state catastrophically. A
deterministic, single-threaded processing loop was chosen over a multi-process or event-driven
model. The 2s latency budget (Requirement 4) makes concurrency unnecessary, and a single
loop guarantees strict reproducibility: identical visual inputs and game states yield identical
control actions.
2.2 Translating the functional analysis into a design
The thirteen Functional Units (FUs) defined in the approved Project Proposal map onto a software
architecture grouped by update frequency rather than subject matter:
• Static Execution Rate (Once per board configuration):The physical maze walls stay
stationary during game runs. The maze graph generation unit (FU5) scans the board once at
setup and caches the navigable occupancy grid, which avoids redundant image processing
during live control.
• Dynamic Execution Rate (Every camera frame):Robot state estimation (FU2, FU3,
FU4), path planning (FU6), defender patrol management (FU7), closed-loop position
correction (FU8), proximity/threat evaluation (FU9, FU10), and game-state monitoring
(FU11) execute on every frame.

• System Interfaces (Loop brackets):Frame capture (FU1) and wireless transmission
(FU12, FU13) bracket the pipeline. Image acquisition (FU1) is the sole pipeline stage that
uses an external driver library, strictly restricted to opening the USB device and grabbing

2.3 Design alternatives and trade-offs considered
Engineered trade-offs were evaluated across several key subsystems:
• Marker Design (Fiducial vs. Custom Two-Dot Marker):Standard fiducial systems
(e.g. ArUco) offer built-in 6-DOF pose estimation [2]. They were rejected because their
detection algorithms rely on external image-processing libraries prohibited by the Project
Proposal. A custom two-dot asymmetric colour marker was selected instead: unique hue
combinations enable agent classification without complex decoding, while two dots of
unequal surface area resolve heading and eliminate 180○orientation ambiguities. The cost
is increased sensitivity to ambient lighting variations.
• Localisation Architecture (Onboard Encoders vs. Overhead Vision):Wheel encoders
on each vehicle would simplify motion control between vision frames. They were inten-
tionally omitted to comply with the sensorless problem definition, accepting the challenge
of purely vision-guided feedback control.
• Path Planning Algorithm (A∗vs. alternatives):The maze has a regular wall spacing
of 150 mm, so a grid is a natural choice. We chose A∗with an admissible octile heuristic
because it finds the shortest path on this grid [4], supporting Requirement 5. Standard RRT
was not chosen because it does not guarantee the shortest path. Potential-field methods
can get stuck in local minima, especially in dead-end corridors.
• Replanning Strategy (Full A∗Replanning vs. Incremental Planners):Incremental
algorithms such as D∗Lite reuse previous search trees to cut re-computation time. They
assume a fixed search start node while updating obstacle locations. Here the measured
starting position of the robot updates dynamically every frame under sensor noise and drift.
Re-anchoring an incremental search tree to a moving start position introduces significant
overhead. Full per-frame A∗replanning was adopted instead, exploiting the embedded
platform’s processing capacity.
• Constraint Formulation (Hard Constraints vs. Soft Penalty Costs):Treating obstacles,
defender threat zones, and partner routes as hard constraints frequently rendered tight maze
scenarios mathematically unsolvable. Soft penalty costs were implemented instead: hard
constraints are reserved exclusively for static maze walls, while clearance preferences,
defender proximity, and route separation enter as additive step costs. The planner can then
degrade gracefully—preferring open space where available, traversing narrow or risky
gaps when no alternative exists.

• Multi-Agent Coordination (Fixed Priority vs. Minimum Travel Assignment):Fixed
priority ordering, where Agent 1 always plans first and Agent 2 avoids Agent 1’s route, was
evaluated and rejected. Fixed ordering makes agent paths cross when the agents start on
opposing sides, raising collision risk. Goal assignment instead minimises total combined
path length, which yields optimal, non-crossing route selection.
2.4 Verification strategy
The qualification testing strategy (Section 4) reflects the prioritised system requirements (Section
4 of the Project Proposal):
1. Integrated System Evaluation:Mission-level requirements such as game win rate are
evaluated on the fully integrated physical platform through automated live trial logging,
not by aggregating isolated subsystem tests.
2. Independent Ground-Truth Baselines:System performance measurements are validated
against independent physical baselines. Path optimality is checked against an exhaust-
ive brute-force grid search; camera position estimates are verified against manual grid
measurements on the physical board.
3. Unit Testing vs. System Qualification:An offline software unit-test suite (≈700checks)

tests stay strictly distinct from qualification tests: unit testing confirms that code executes as
designed, whereas qualification testing proves that the system meets external engineering

3.5.2 Locating the board
The board must be located before any measurement can be expressed in millimetres. The board
is the darkest large region in the frame, so an intensity threshold separating board from table is
obtained by Otsu’s method applied to the intensity field. Each of the four board edges is then
fitted as a straight line in image coordinates, and the pixel-to-millimetre mapping follows from
those four lines by linear interpolation across the board.
The mapping is not a single scale factor, however. The scale varies across the image because
the lens is not perfectly rectilinear and the board is not perfectly square to the sensor, so the
horizontal scale is evaluated at the image row of the point being converted. Expressing every
geometric parameter in millimetres and converting to pixels at runtime, rather than storing any
quantity in pixels, is a rule applied across the whole project, since a tolerance expressed in pixels
cannot be compared against a requirement expressed in millimetres.
If the board runs off the top and bottom of the frame simultaneously the vertical datum is
undefined and the vertical coordinate cannot be recovered. This condition is detected and
reported rather than allowed to produce a map containing nothing but the board border.

3.5.3 Segmenting the marker dots
Candidate marker pixels are those whose chroma exceeds a floor. A single global floor was
used initially and it failed, because the four marker colours are not equally saturated: the printed
green marker measured a median chroma of 34 against a global gate of 35. The green dot had
therefore shattered into fragments too small to accept, and a robot plainly visible in the frame
was discarded.
The segmentation is therefore performed per colour band. A pixel qualifies if its chroma exceeds
the global floor,orif its hue lies within a particular colour’s band and its chroma exceeds that
colour’s own floor. The per-colour floors are 45 for red, 50 for orange, 30 for green and 60 for
blue. Only bands whose floor lies below the global gate are able to widen the candidate set, so
the colours that were already segmenting correctly are left unaffected.
The underlying fault is worth stating in general terms, because it recurred three times in this
project in different modules:the gate that forms a connected region was stricter than the gate
that accepts it. A colour required to reach a chroma of 30 to be accepted could not form a region
below 35, so it could never be accepted at all.

3.5.4 Connected-component labelling
Candidate pixels are grouped into connected regions by four-connected labelling, written from
first principles. Four-connectivity rather than eight is used because two regions meeting at a
single diagonal pixel are more likely to be two objects than one.
The labelling was first implemented as a breadth-first flood fill, which is the clearer formulation
however it visits every candidate pixel from an interpreted loop. On the embedded platform
this had cost 947 ms of a 1380 ms full-resolution wall detection. It was replaced by a run-based
algorithm, in which horizontal runs of candidate pixels are extracted by array operations and
only the runs, of which there are a few thousand rather than a few hundred thousand, are walked.
Two runs on adjacent rows belong to the same component when their column spans overlap,
which is exactly four-connectivity, and a union-find structure merges them.
The procedure is set out in Algorithm 1. The two passes are separated deliberately: the first
assigns provisional labels and records equivalences, and the second resolves each provisional
label to the representative of its set, so that a component discovered to be a single region only on
its last row is still labelled consistently on its first.
The rewrite reduced labelling from 947 ms to 193 ms on a real frame and the full wall detection
from 1380 ms to 320 ms. The flood fill was retained as a reference implementation, and the
two were verified to agree on 120 random masks, on ten shapes chosen specifically to break a
run-based labeller, and on a real full-resolution frame, where both produced 528 components
with bit-identical label images.

3.7.2 Scanning with the robots present
A scan must not record a robot as a wall, so the region each robot occupies is excluded before
labelling. That exclusion creates a second problem. The wall mask is then empty where the
robots are, and a mask that is empty is indistinguishable from floor. Rasterised naively this

direction in which to be wrong: a phantom gap routes a robot into a wall, whereas a phantom
wall only costs drivable area.

being wall, free and unknown. Several looks at the board are combined and each look votes only

3.8.3 Smoothing
The grid path is a sequence of cell centres and contains many short collinear or near-collinear
segments, so it is shortened by string pulling. The smoother is greedy rather than pairwise: from
the current waypoint it looks as far ahead as the route runs and keeps the furthest waypoint that
can still be reached directly, then continues from there. A pairwise rule that deletes a waypoint
whenever its two neighbours are mutually visible removes one corner at a time and leaves the
staircase largely intact, whereas taking the furthest admissible waypoint collapses a whole run of
cells into a single leg in one pass.
A shortcut is admissible on three tests. It must be free of obstacles; its minimum clearance must
be no worse than that of the stretch it replaces; and the worst threat cost along it must be no worse
either. The second test is measured along the path rather than against a fixed floor, so a route
through a genuinely tight gap may still be straightened. However, it may not be made tighter than
it already had to be. Without that test the smoother undoes the centring the search has just paid
for, because pulling a path straight is precisely what moves it back against the inside of a bend.
The third test exists for the same reason applied to the oracle lens of Section 3.10: a shortcut that
saves twenty millimetres by cutting across a defender’s line of sight is not an improvement.
The smoother is stated in Algorithm 3. The clearance test in the admissibility condition is what
distinguishes it from a plain string-pulling pass: the shortcut is compared against the stretch it
would replace rather than against a fixed floor, so the smoother can never make a route tighter
than it already had to be.

3.9 The position correction policy
3.9.1 Derivation from the differential-drive kinematics
Each robot is a differential-drive vehicle. The kinematic model below rests on four assumptions,
each of which is reasonable for this platform however each of which is also a limitation of the

directly to ground speed; the chassis is assumed rigid and its motion planar, so that the pose is

compared with the motor time constant.

3.9.2 Separating turning from tracking
The steering law (13) is a small-angle correction and is not appropriate for acquiring a waypoint
that lies behind the robot, so a hysteresis band is applied. A robot whose heading error exceeds
35○turns on the spot until the error falls below 20○, after which it drives forward under (13). The
two thresholds differ so that a robot oscillating about the boundary does not alternate between
turning and driving on successive frames.
3.9.3 Following the route rather than chasing the waypoint
A* guarantees that the route is free of obstacles and the smoother guarantees that each segment of
it is. However, neither guarantees anything about a straight line drawn from wherever the robot
has actually drifted to. This distinction was the cause of several collisions, in which a robot that
had drifted laterally aimed directly at its next waypoint and the straight line from its displaced
position to that waypoint cut across a wall. In two recorded instances the robot was following its
commanded line to within 0.7 mm and 2.5 mm while that line passed 10 mm and 20 mm inside a
wall.
The correction layer therefore evaluates the line it is about to drive. If the minimum clearance
along that line is worse than the clearance the robot currently has, the robot aims instead at a
point on the planned segment, one robot length ahead of its own projection onto that segment.
The planned segment is already known to be clear, so only the short lateral hop back onto it is
unverified.
The comparison is made against the robot’s present clearance rather than against zero. A robot
whose own position lies inside the inflated margin, which is normal at a base cell whose centre is
only 16 mm from the inflated board edge, has no line out of that position with positive clearance,
so comparing against zero would refuse every such line and the robot could never leave. The
rule implemented is instead that a move may not make clearance worse than it already is, which
permits escape while still forbidding further intrusion.

3.10 Proximity detection and the capture condition
3.10.1 The capture condition
An attacker is captured when a defender is simultaneously close enough, pointed at it, and able
to see it. All three conditions are required:
• the separation is at most the capture radius of 165 mm;
• the attacker lies within the defender’s field of view of 60○, that is within 30○either side of
its heading;
• the straight line between them is unobstructed by a wall.
Proximity alone would make passing behind a defender fatal, which is not the stated game, while
omitting the line-of-sight test would let a defender see through walls. The line-of-sight test uses
the uninflated wall map, because the planner’s inflation is approximately the width of a robot
and a robot’s width does not stop light; using the inflated map would make defenders blind along
corridors that are optically open.
Figure 18 draws the three radii to scale with the field of view, and the scale is what makes the
ordering of (15) legible. The avoidance radius sits inside the capture radius, which sits inside
the sense radius, so the gaps between the three circles are the margins the design depends on.

3.10.2 The ordering of the radii
Three radii govern the game, and their ordering is a design constraint rather than a matter of
taste:
Ravoid<R capture<R sense,(15)
with the values 130 mm, 165 mm and 200 mm respectively.
The lower bound is necessary for the game to be playable at all. The planner treats every robot
as an obstacle within Ravoid, so if Rcapture≤Ravoid the attackers’ own obstacle avoidance holds
them permanently outside capture range and capture becomes geometrically impossible. This
condition had been violated in an earlier version of the design, with a capture radius of 100 mm
against an avoidance radius of 130 mm. Over 13014 frames of a recorded run the closest a
defender came to an attacker was 151 mm, and the capture test was never once presented with a
case that could return true, which left requirement 1 unfalsifiable because there was nothing to
avoid.
The upper bound is necessary for evasion to be a decision rather than luck. If Rcapture≥Rsense
there exists a band in which an attacker is catchable but cannot detect the defender responsible,
and no amount of planning is able to help.
The two bounds fix an open interval however they do not fix a value. Writing the two margins
as m1=R capture−Ravoid and m2=R sense−Rcapture, the design is robust to whichever margin is the
smaller, so the quantity to maximise is min(m1,m 2). As Rcapture increases, m1 increases and m2

3.10.3 Oracle lens
The detection radius belongs to the attacker and not to the system. An attacker may plan around
a defender only if that defender lies within Rsense=200 mmandis in line of sight. An earlier
implementation had built the threat field from every defender on the board, which granted the
attacker omniscience, and a route that avoids a defender the attacker cannot possibly perceive is
not evasion.
The same restriction applies to the obstacle list and not only to the threat field, and the distinction
is worth stating because the two are separate paths into the planner. An attacker plans around
its partner and around those defenders it perceives, and it does not plan around a defender it
has no means of detecting, in either the obstacle list or the threat field. The navigator, which
is responsible for not colliding with anything, retains the full list of robots, because collision
avoidance is a question of where a body is and not of what the attacker knows. This separation
is safe precisely because of (15), since a defender close enough to matter as an obstacle, at
Ravoid=130 mm, is already inside both the capture radius and the sense radius and is therefore
necessarily perceived unless the line of sight is blocked.
Perceived defenders contribute an additive cost τ to the planner’s step cost in (8), over a neigh-
bourhood of radius 300 mm. The cost is 8 for a cell currently inside a defender’s capture cone,
5 for a cell on the defender’s near-term patrol route, and 3 for a cell merely within the oracle
radius. These accumulate rather than taking a maximum, because a cell that is both visible to
a defender and on its path is genuinely worse than one that is only either. The neighbourhood
extends beyond the capture radius because both robots move, and avoiding only where a defender
is, rather than where it will be, is insufficient at the speeds involved.
Figure 19 draws what an attacker can perceive against what a defender can capture, from one
position, and the margin between the two is the answer to the obvious objection. If an attacker
could be captured from further away than it can detect, it would be caught by defenders it had
no means of avoiding and the evasion would be unimplementable rather than merely difficult.
The figure shows that the sense region contains the capture region with room to spare, so every
capture is in principle avoidable, which is what makes the danger field a meaningful input rather
than decoration.

3.10.4 When to replan
Replanning is triggered only when a perceived defender actually threatens the attacker, which
is a narrower condition than proximity. The three triggering conditions are that the defender
can currently see the attacker, that it is facing the attacker within the detection radius, or that its
near-term patrol route intersects the attacker’s remaining route within capture range.
A defender passing behind the attacker, or alongside it facing away, or whose patrol runs parallel
to the attacker’s route, is not a threat and does not trigger a replan. The narrower trigger was
adopted after an attacker had replanned 39 times in a single 300 s run under a proximity-only
trigger and had committed to no route at all.

3.11 Two-attacker coordination
Section 2.1 of the Project Proposal identifies the coordination of the two attackers as a primary
design challenge, on the grounds that independent path finding causes both robots to select the
same shortest path and deadlock. That failure was reproduced on the physical system, in that
planning from recorded start positions with each attacker independently choosing its nearest goal
cell sent both attackers to the same cell. Two mechanisms were implemented in response.

3.11.1 Distinct goal assignment
The opposing base consists of two cells, and the two attackers are assigned one cell each by
whichever of the two possible pairings minimises the total straight-line travel. With two agents
and two goals there are exactly two pairings, so exhaustive comparison is not a heuristic but the
optimum. Ties are broken by a fixed ordering on the robot names, so that the two robots cannot
compute different assignments and set off for each other’s cell.
Figure 21 draws both pairings from one recorded pair of start positions, with the total travel
marked on each. The crossing pairing is the one a fixed priority produces whenever the robots
happen to start on the wrong sides, and the figure shows what is wrong with it: the two routes
intersect near the middle of the board, which is where the corridors are narrowest and where two
robots meeting head-on have no room to pass. The chosen pairing has the shorter total and, more
usefully, does not intersect at all.

3.11.2 Route separation by cost
Assignment alone does not prevent the two attackers from using the same corridor, so a partner’s
committed route contributes an additive cost to the planner, falling linearly from 4 at the route to
zero at 150 mm from it. This cost is added to the threat cost of (8) rather than replacing it, since
a corridor that is both shared with a partner and watched by a defender is worse than one that is
only either.
The magnitude is deliberately smaller than the oracle costs of 8 and 5. Being captured ends the
run whereas sharing a corridor merely risks it, so an attacker will accept a shared corridor in
order to escape a defender but will not accept a defender in order to avoid sharing a corridor, and
the ordering of these three constants is what encodes that priority.

weight, the proportion of one attacker’s route lying within 150 mm of the other’s fell from 44% to
8%, at a cost of 317 mm of additional route length. An earlier measurement of the same quantity,

3.11.3 Vacating the goal
An attacker that has reached the base has completed its objective and becomes an obstacle. In
a recorded run the first attacker had arrived after 109 s and had stopped on the base cell, and

the recorded positions confirms the mechanism, in that either obstacle alone left a route whereas
the two together left none.

with the arrived robot at each candidate position, rather than a proxy such as a fixed distance
from the base. A proxy was implemented first and it did not work, because a position satisfying
a 170 mm separation from every base cell still blocked the partner: being far from the base is not
the same thing as being out of the way.

3.11.4 The deadlock-free condition
The coordination strategy guarantees the following three properties.
Goal contention cannot occur .The assignment is a bijection between attackers and base cells
and both attackers compute it identically from the same inputs, so no two attackers ever hold the
same goal.
Coordination cannot remove a feasible route.Both the partner separation cost and the clearance
cost are finite additive terms in the step cost of (8) and are not constraints on the search, so A*
explores exactly the same set of feasible paths with or without them and returns no route only
if no route exists in the underlying graph. Coordination is therefore able to lengthen a route
however it cannot make a solvable instance unsolvable.
The goal region does not remain occupied.An arrived attacker vacates to a position from which
its partner’s route is feasible, if such a position exists within reach, and if none exists this is
detected and reported rather than silently ignored.

3.13 Integration into a single pipeline
All functional units execute in a single loop on the central processor, at one iteration per
camera frame. Each iteration captures a frame, locates the board, finds and classifies the robots,
updates the game state, plans or revalidates a route for each attacker, issues the defenders’ patrol
waypoints, and computes and transmits one motor command per robot.
42

=== PDF PAGE 61 ===
A.W. Du Toit Part 3. Main Report
Exactly one decision is taken per robot per frame. The base station expires a command after
500 ms and a robot stops after 300 ms without one, so a control loop that dies, blocks, or loses
sight of the board leaves the robots stopped without having to send anything at all. This is the
reason commands are issued as a single transmission per frame rather than as a timed sequence,
since safety is then obtained from the absence of commands rather than from the delivery of a
stop.
Commands are transmitted without waiting for an acknowledgement. Waiting for acknow-
ledgements had been measured to reduce the loop from 19 to 8 iterations per second, and the
acknowledgement carries no information that the next frame will not supply in any case.
The loop processes a decimated frame for robot tracking and retains the full-resolution frame for
wall scanning. At a decimation factor of five the loop achieves 13.5 iterations per second with
a processing time of approximately 25 ms per frame, whereas at full resolution it achieves 1.0.
The decimation costs approximately 1.2 mm of position accuracy, which is acceptable against
the 25 mm requirement, whereas the frame rate is not negotiable: at 4 iterations per second a
robot turning at 50○/s rotates 12.5○between decisions against a 12○tolerance.
Two further stages are run at a reduced rate rather than every frame, and in both cases the reason
is that their cost is comparable with the whole frame budget. The colour-blind fallback, which
recovers a robot whose marker colour has been lost entirely, was measured at 79 ms against a
frame of approximately 100 ms, so it is executed on every fifth iteration. Encoding and publishing
the two operator images costs approximately 80 ms, so they are published on every third iteration.
Neither stage contributes to the control decision, and running either on every frame would
roughly halve the rate at which the robots are steered in order to improve something that is not
steering them.
A failed frame grab is counted rather than ignored. Five consecutive failures stop the fleet and
the condition is reported, and the loop terminates rather than continuing to spin once the failures
persist. The hazard being guarded against is the same one the watchdog below addresses, which
is a fleet executing its last command against a position estimate that is no longer being refreshed.
Rescanning the board is performed from within this loop without stopping the robots. The frames
of a scan are collected one per iteration and the detection work is performed on a separate thread,
so the control loop continues to steer throughout. A scan completes in approximately 3.7 s and
the loop rate falls from 13.7 to 8.8 iterations per second while it runs.
The camera is monitored by a watchdog. If no frame arrives for 3 s the fleet is stopped and the
condition is reported, because the hazard of a failed camera is not the lost frames themselves but
the robots continuing to execute their last command against a position estimate that has stopped
being true.
