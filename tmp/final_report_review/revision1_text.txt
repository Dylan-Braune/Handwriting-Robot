1. Literature study
1.1 Handwriting recognition and writer identification
1.1.1 Offline recognition and available datasets
The ability of a computer or system to identify written text after being trained on large amounts
of data is known as HWR (Handwriting Recognition) or HTR (Handwritten Text Recognition) [1,
p. 39]. One type of handwritten text recognition is offline recognition, which means the data
being identified by the system is static and captured post-completion of the page of handwritten
text. This is ideal for identifying the text from a fixed image of handwritten content by utilising
image processing techniques and algorithms to identify the text that was written [2, p. 268]. The
other type is online recognition, which is a more dynamic process that uses information recorded
during the creation of the handwriting. The addition of a time variable includes data about the
order of strokes as well as pen-up or pen-down movements [3, p. 18].
This project uses offline recognition because its inputs are photographed or scanned pages. The
text and writer must therefore be identified from visible ink geometry. The absence of recorded
stroke order also influences how the handwriting can later be reproduced: a path recovered from
an image is a geometric estimate, rather than a recording of the writer’s movements.
Multiple datasets have been used for training handwritten text recognition modules. The EMNIST
(Extended MNIST) By_Class dataset consists of 814 255 handwritten characters and numbers,
each being 28 by 28 pixels in size [ 1, p. 40]. There is only a single character per image,
which suits isolated-character classification but does not itself supply examples of connected
handwritten words. The IAM handwriting dataset provides complete handwritten English pages,
lines and words with their transcriptions and writer metadata [ 4]. Hemanth et al. report the
expanded database as 5 685 labelled sentences and 115 320 labelled words from 657 writers [5,
p. 2460].
The dataset influences which variations the classifier encounters and which labels are available.
For this project, line images and their transcripts support recognition without manually labelling
every character boundary. Writer-labelled pages also support author classification and extraction
of individual style profiles. IAM supplies public handwriting variation, while the personal dataset
represents the project’s photographed input conditions. A small isolated-character dataset alone
would not address both requirements.
1.1.2 Image conditioning and text separation
The image conditioning of handwriting pages includes techniques performed on the handwritten
text before it is passed into the classification and feature-recognition algorithms. This accounts for
dimensional adjustments, removal of inconsistencies and normalisation of the data, to make the
images more appropriate for text separation and classification [5, pp. 2458–2460]. Normalisation
reduces the influence of unwanted variation; examples include min–max scaling and z-score
normalisation [1, pp. 40–41].

Binarisation differentiates the background page from the ink of the written text, computed
by pixel. A global threshold taken from the grey-level histogram uses one decision level for
the image [6]. A local threshold instead uses statistics from a window around each pixel and
can accommodate uneven illumination [7, pp. 2–3]. Deskewing corrects page rotation, while
deslanting corrects the lean of individual characters by applying a horizontal shear. These are
different transformations: aligning text lines does not require removing the writer’s characteristic
slant [8, 9].
For the camera input in this project, illumination correction and deskewing are needed before
the ink is separated into lines. Slant must remain available as a writer-specific measurement; it
can be removed for glyph storage and reapplied when the text is synthesised. The purpose is to
suppress capture variation while retaining the information needed for reproduction.
Text separation is the process of breaking up a page of handwritten text into smaller segments or
blocks that can be used in feature extraction and neural-network classifiers. Existing methods
separate text into lines, words or individual characters. Hemanth et al. obtain lines and words
through filtering, dilation and bounding boxes [ 5, pp. 2459–2460]. When lines are mostly
straight and separated by blank rows, boundaries can be found from minima in the row ink
profile. Where ascenders and descenders overlap, a boundary that bends between lines is more
suitable. Minimum-cost seam methods provide a way to trace such curved boundaries [10, p. 3].
The project therefore separates whole lines before recognition. This avoids requiring a reliable
cut between every pair of connected letters. Character boundaries are still needed to construct a
glyph library, but there the known transcript and a trained line reader can constrain the extraction.
Line segmentation and glyph extraction consequently solve different problems, even though both
divide an image.
1.1.3 Neural networks and feature extraction
Many different types of neural-network models have been implemented for handwriting recog-
nition, including artificial neural networks (ANNs), convolutional neural networks (CNNs),
recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and combinations of them. ANNs consist of interconnected
neurons, weights, hidden layers and biases that learn a mapping from the input to an output.
CNNs use local connections and shared kernels to extract spatial patterns with fewer independent
parameters than an otherwise comparable fully connected image layer [ 11, pp. 6–8]. RNNs
maintain a state across a sequence, allowing a prediction to depend on neighbouring evidence [2,
p. 269].
The convolutional layers of a CNN perform feature extraction by sliding a convolutional kernel
over the image. Each local input value is multiplied by a corresponding kernel value, the products
are summed, and a bias and activation function produce an output feature value. Reusing the
kernel allows the same pattern to be detected at different locations [ 11, p. 6]. For unit stride
and no padding, an N by N input with an f by f kernel returns an (N−f+1) by (N−f+1)
feature map. Pooling then reduces spatial resolution and retains selected responses [11, pp. 6–7].
Figure 1 illustrates these operations.
The LeNet-5 model is an established example of this structure. It uses a 32×32input and seven
trainable layers, including convolution, subsampling and fully connected stages; its original
output uses radial-basis units [11, pp. 7–8]. A deeper alternative is a residual network, whose

A combination of CNNs and recurrent layers allows an entire line to be read without word or
character separation. The convolutional layers convert the cropped line into feature columns,
and a bidirectional recurrent layer reads the sequence in both directions, providing context from
before and after each position [ 12, pp. 4–5]. In this offline setting, the sequence axis is the
horizontal position in the image, rather than the measured time at which a pen moved. The full
line crop is available before classification. Related convolutional–recurrent recognition systems
use the same distinction between image features and sequence prediction [13, pp. 2–3].
Long sequences make simple recurrent networks difficult to train because gradients can diminish
or grow through repeated updates. Long short-term memory (LSTM) uses an additive memory
state and gates to control what is retained, replaced and exposed [3, pp. 4–5]. The convolutional
feature columns usually outnumber the transcript characters, so their alignment is initially
unknown. Connectionist temporal classification (CTC) adds a blank symbol and sums the
probabilities of the paths consistent with the transcript. Repeated adjacent symbols are collapsed
before blanks are removed [14, pp. 2–4]. Figure 2 shows the resulting recognition path.

1.1.4 Training and assessment
The way networks are trained corresponds to the architecture they are built from. Batch normal-
isation subtracts the batch mean and divides by the standard deviation, with a stabilising constant
and learned scale and offset [15, p. 4]. Dropout reduces overfitting by removing a random subset
of a layer’s outputs during training, limiting dependence on individual features [16, pp. 1931,
1934]. Data augmentation introduces small rotations, shears or smooth distortions while retaining
the transcription [17]. These methods address different sources of variation and do not replace
separate validation data.
Recognition is commonly assessed through character error rate (CER) or word error rate (WER),
using insertions, deletions and substitutions relative to a reference transcript [ 14, p. 2]. An
error in one character can spoil a whole word, but word edit error is not simply a count of
words at matching positions. The metric and its reference population must remain explicit when
comparing systems.
For this project, numerical forward passes, backward passes and optimiser updates make the
learning calculations inspectable. A corresponding library implementation supports training
and comparison, with stored arrays used for NumPy inference on the ODROID. The report
distinguishes the selected library-trained weights from the independently trained numerical
models. Character edits, writer decisions and physical drawing measurements are evaluated
separately because they measure different functions.
1.1.5 Techniques for writer identification
There are multiple approaches to identifying the author of a written piece of text. One uses
measured, text-independent features such as slant, height, stroke width, spacing and connectivity.
Another describes the distribution of edge directions and recurring letter fragments, giving a
texture and shape description of the writing [18, p. 701]. Alternatively, a neural network learns
discriminative features from examples labelled by writer [19]. Such a classifier identifies a writer
among its trained classes; it does not automatically identify an unfamiliar person.
This project needs an author classifier to select and assess the stored writer styles, while explicit
geometry measurements are needed to reconstruct their appearance. Ink-trace width can itself
contain writer information [20, p. 162], but the gantry uses its own pen. Stroke normalisation is
therefore applied to reduce reliance on that cue and direct the classifier towards shape. Writer
identity and stylistic fidelity remain separate measures: recognising an author does not establish
that every letter or spacing feature has been reproduced accurately.
1.2 Handwriting synthesis in a writer’s style
Before handwritten text can be reproduced through hardware, the desired text must be created in
the selected author’s style. Four approaches illustrate the trade-offs between source data, learned
variation and the form of the output.

Recurrent stroke-level synthesis.A recurrent neural network predicts the next pen movement
through small coordinate offsets and an end-of-stroke indicator. It models a distribution of
possible movements because no letter is written identically every time [3, pp. 18–20]. Its output
is naturally suited to a plotter, but training uses online recordings. This project’s offline pages
contain no measured stroke order, so this approach would require a different source dataset and
an additional generative model.
Adversarial image synthesis.A generative adversarial network trains a generator and discrimin-
ator together: one generates handwriting, while the other helps distinguish generated samples
from real examples. GANwriting also constrains content and writer style to produce text im-
ages [21, pp. 3–7]. Once trained, the generator produces an image in a forward pass. This is a
relevant alternative for learning contextual letterforms, but a raster output still needs conversion
into the pen paths required by this project.
Diffusion-based synthesis.This method learns to reverse a noise-corruption process and gener-
ates handwriting through successive denoising steps. One-DM uses one reference style image
at generation time, while its training uses a much larger handwriting corpus [ 22, pp. 3–5, 9].
A small reference requirement must therefore be distinguished from the data and computation
required to train the model. For this project, diffusion would add both a separate training task
and image-to-path recovery, rather than directly supplying the motion representation.
Glyph-library synthesis.This method assembles words from shapes or glyphs extracted from
existing handwritten pages. Haines et al. store labelled forms and select and join them using
information about their shape, context and spacing [23, pp. 4–8]. Their standard data collection
uses approximately four A4 pages per writer [23, p. 4]. This suits the project’s small personal
collections and allows explicit control of size, slant and spacing. The main limitation is that
a finite collection of character shapes and joining rules only approximates the way connected
handwriting changes with its neighbours.
Generated handwriting can be assessed through geometric comparisons, recognition and human
judgement. Haines et al. include human evaluation, while image-generation work also uses text
and writer recognition to assess the output [23, pp. 12–14], [21, pp. 8–9]. For the present robot,
model scores help choose a candidate before drawing, but final physical output also needs its
own read-back and geometric assessment. A classifier used to select the candidate is not an
independent human judgement of its style.
1.3 Physical reproduction and pen-path mapping
There are three relevant families of mechanism for reproducing text on paper: Cartesian systems,
serial-chain robots and parallel-chain robots. A Cartesian system consists of a gantry with
independent x–y movement. Serial-chain robots consist of linkages connected in a chain, like
a typical robot arm. Parallel mechanisms use several linked chains to support the moving
platform [24, pp. 5–8]. These arrangements differ in their workspace, mechanical structure and
coordinate transformations.
A Cartesian gantry is suitable here because the page is flat and the main motion requirement
is two-dimensional. Each axis can be commanded and calibrated separately, with a further
pen-lift mechanism for travel. This makes the conversion from an image-derived path to physical

A pen path is stored as a list of points, and a recovered stroke usually has more points than a
plotter needs. Polyline simplification reduces this list while retaining the stroke’s shape. The
Douglas–Peucker method joins the endpoints, finds the point furthest from that line and retains it
when its distance exceeds a chosen tolerance, then repeats on the resulting parts [25]. A small
tolerance follows the stroke closely; a large tolerance removes more points but can cut corners.
When handwriting exists only as an image, the path must first be recovered, for example by
thinning the ink to a centreline [26, p. 237].
These operations are needed to turn the project’s stored glyphs into manageable G-code trajector-
ies. Simplification controls geometric detail, while the thinning and tracing determine which
strokes are available to the synthesiser. Neither recovers the original writing speed or pressure
from a photograph, so the controller must define its own drawing feed and pen transitions.
There are multiple methods of linear actuation that could be considered for a Cartesian plotting
system, including belt drives, rack-and-pinion drives and screw drives [27]. The project uses
timing belts and pulleys to convert motor rotation into movement along supported rails. This
choice prioritises a straightforward, affordable page-sized mechanism with sufficient travel speed;
its nominal step resolution must still be distinguished from measured positioning accuracy.
1.4 Application of the literature
The literature supports a system built around offline pages, complete-line recognition and explicit
glyph geometry. Image conditioning and curved line separation prepare photographed input;
convolutional features support separate text and writer outputs; transcript-guided extraction
turns observed handwriting into reusable paths. A glyph library then supplies controllable
geometry without training an additional handwriting generator. The Cartesian gantry provides
the physical output stage. Section 2 explains how these choices were combined and developed,
while Section 3 gives their calculations, implementation and verification.

2. Approach
The project was approached as two interacting systems: one interprets photographed handwriting
and constructs writer-specific paths, while the other converts those paths into physical pen
movement. The proposal’s functional analysis provides their responsibilities; the G-code interface
allows their implementation and verification to proceed separately.
2.1 From functional analysis to concept
The first concept retained page acquisition (FU 1), processing on the single-board computer
(FU 2), the gantry (FU 3) and the power supply (FU 4). The final arrangement is shown in
Figures 3 and 4. A restrained page and fixed camera supply the image. Conditioning and
line segmentation prepare it for two outputs: a transcript from the sequence reader and a
writer identity from the author classifier. Known transcripts separately support glyph and style
extraction, producing persistent profiles from which new text can be synthesised.
The proposal’s character-separation concept was developed into line-level recognition because
joined handwriting does not supply a reliable boundary between every letter. Character boundar-
ies are recovered during profile construction, where the known text constrains the problem. The
writer network uses the same convolutional architecture as the reader with a different head and
separate weights. Its input is stroke-normalised so the robot’s pen appearance has less influence
on writer identification.
The chosen physical concept is a belt-driven Cartesian gantry with two stepper motors and a
separate pen-lift mechanism. The Y-axis motor drives both side belts through a cross-shaft.
The ODROID coordinates the axes, monitors the switches and operates the pen motor through
a transistor. This keeps planar movement directly related to the requested coordinates, while
calibration establishes the usable travel and step scale.
2.2 Development and verification sequence
The motion and processing subsystems meet at a file of G-code. This interface allows geometric
commands to exercise the gantry independently of recognition and synthesis, and allows gener-
ated trajectories to be inspected before physical execution. Mechanical assembly, belt alignment,
the pen mechanism and homing could therefore be developed and checked without requiring a
trained handwriting model.
The processing design is presented in dependency order: page conditioning and line separation;
text and writer classification; then glyph extraction, layout and trajectory generation. The line
reader is needed for transcript-guided glyph alignment, and the resulting stored geometry is
needed before synthesis can assemble new text. Intermediate images, numerical layer checks
and independently parsed G-code provide checks at these interfaces. Formal qualification is then
organised by the proposal’s six requirements in Section 4.
The implementation includes a NumPy training and inference route and a corresponding library
training route. Off-device training supplies the selected library-trained joint reader and eleven-
writer model, while inference and motion control run on the ODROID. The numerical models are

3.3 Hardware design
The hardware must hold the paper and pen securely, reach the required writing area, and convert
a stored trajectory into repeatable motion. The design therefore combines a Cartesian belt drive
with a separately actuated pen mechanism. The electrical interface is kept simple enough for
direct control from the ODROID, while calibration relates software coordinates to the travel of
the assembled machine.
3.3.1 Plotter mechanics and mechanical construction
The chosen method of text plotting is a Cartesian gantry system with two independent axes of
movement and a pen up/down mechanism, consistent with the Cartesian arrangement illustrated
by Teikari [24, p. 6]. Complex kinematic equations and joint-dependent position errors are
avoided by using separate x and y movements for reproduction. Linear GT2 timing belts and
pulleys are used to convert rotary motion from stepper motors and axles to the desired Cartesian
movements. Timing belts were chosen for their cost effectiveness and ability to provide relatively
fast travel without a lead-screw transmission.
A Cartesian gantry consists of stationary rails that guide movement in one axis, and a movable
rail that guides the other axis of movement. For this system and the explanations that follow, the
stationary rails guide y-axis movement, while the x-axis rail moves along the y-axis. Figure 5
shows the axes of the gantry.
The x-axis rail needs to move with stability and consistency, which is why it is supported at both
ends by the two y-axis rails. A single stepper motor was chosen to drive the pulleys on both sides
to ensure synchronised motion and save costs. Two independent stepper motors driving one axis
introduce a synchronisation concern because differences in missed steps or loading could offset
one side relative to the other. The challenge with using one motor to drive two timing pulleys
is extending its shaft to both pulleys without excessive load or twist in the new axle. A rigid
coupler was used to extend the stepper motor shaft with a chromed steel rod.
To reduce strain on the motors and allow smoother movement, linear bearings were fitted around
the chromed rod rails so that the moving parts slide with low resistance. Bearing alignment is
important as well as bearing diameter: an incorrectly aligned bearing can constrain the same rod
in a different direction from the neighbouring bearing, increasing resistance instead of reducing

3.3.2 Stepper motor selection and drive resolution
A stepper motor turns through fixed angular increments as the winding-current pattern changes.
The windings are the motor’s coils, and each current pattern establishes a magnetic field that
pulls the rotor towards a corresponding position. The A4988 regulates these winding currents
by chopping: it switches the drive to maintain the requested current level. Chopping continues
even while a position is held; advancing the requested current pattern, rather than chopping itself,
produces the next step [28, p. 9].
Two stepper motors are used in the gantry plotter, one for the x and one for the y direction. The
selected motor is the 42BYGH40-01 NEMA 17 bipolar stepper, with a 40 mm body length, a
1.8○full-step angle and a rated current of 1.68 A per phase. Stepper control suits the required

3.3.3 Pen lift mechanism and drive circuit
The pen has two required states: in contact with the page for a stroke, and clear of the page for
travel. A compact geared motor and cam provide these states without adding a third positioning
axis. The motor turns in one direction and the cam alternates the pen height as it rotates. A
switch indicates the raised state. Compared with a servo, this arrangement uses a simple switched
output and position indication, but it gives no commanded intermediate pen height or force.
Figure 6 shows the two pen positions. The holder and page support are part of the same design
decision: repeatable motor movement is of little value if a flexible holder changes the nib position
or if a stack of paper changes contact pressure. A single restrained sheet on a rigid support
reduces this variation. The mechanism was adjusted experimentally to achieve clearance during
travel without excessive pressure during drawing.
Figure 6. Cam-operated mechanism with the pen lowered and raised.

no pulse is required; an unnecessary rotation could move the pen away from the requested state.

feedback operation differs from the G-code generator’s settling dwell, which is described in

3.4 Page conditioning and line segmentation
The recogniser requires an image containing one line of text. A photograph, however, may
contain uneven illumination, a rotated page, printed rules, page edges and strokes extending
between neighbouring lines. The purpose of this stage is to remove those disturbances while
preserving the shape and relative spacing of the handwriting. The implemented sequence in
segment.py works mainly on a half-resolution image, then returns to the full-resolution image

3.4.5 Ink ownership and classifier crop preparation
Seams initially divide the image into bands, but a pixel-by-pixel cut can separate a dot from its
stem or clip a curved descender. Connected-component ownership therefore refines the band
assignment. Components are found with row runs and union operations, following connected-
component labelling principles [36, pp. 476–479]. The implementation includes diagonal contact,
giving eight-connected components. Large pieces can still contain writing from more than one
line, so component labelling alone is not a complete line assignment.
Algorithm 3 summarises the correction order. For a component spanning several bands, a
band holding at least 15% of its in-band ink is substantial. The uppermost substantial band
supplies the reference owner for small-piece corrections; otherwise the largest overlap is used.
Large components are not indiscriminately moved into that band: their pixels retain seam-band
assignment except where the small-piece and narrow-tail rules apply. This distinction preserves
a word’s main body while recovering a protruding part.
An i dot between two text lines illustrates the need for this sequence. A seam may put it with
the upper line because of its vertical position, while a stem below supplies evidence for the
lower line. Closing used for component analysis can also join the dot to an unrelated descender;
the raw mask is therefore revisited rather than treating the closed component as unquestionable
evidence. A ring-shaped mark needs the additional hole test so that an open hook is not treated
as a detached loop. These are geometric decisions with ambiguous cases, rather than knowledge
of the intended character.

3.5 Neural network design, training and classification
The classification stage has two outputs: the text in a line and the identity of its writer. Both
depend on stroke features, but text recognition must preserve their horizontal order while writer
identification must combine evidence across the line. Two networks therefore use the same
convolutional architecture with different heads. The recogniser follows the CNN–BiLSTM
approach of Kizilirmak and Yanikoglu [12, pp. 4–5]; the project implements and integrates that
architecture with its own preprocessing, training and handwriting-reproduction pipeline.
3.5.1 Training data, labels and evaluation separation
Public IAM handwriting [ 4] and the photographed personal contributions provide examples
with known text and writer identity. Public line images supply text-recognition training data,
while writer-labelled pages and the personal samples support writer identification and style
extraction. The eleven writers selected for the reported evaluation and intended demonstration
are six database writers (150, 152, 153, 384, 552 and 588) and five personal contributors. A stored
profile, a classifier output class and an evaluated writer are different objects; the checkpoint’s
recorded class mapping defines how its output indices are interpreted.
The data pipeline pairs every crop with its transcript and writer label. The same preparation
functions are used by the first-principles and framework training routes so that a comparison does
not silently change segmentation or label handling. Blank or unusable labelled lines are excluded.
Characters outside the configured alphabet are filtered by the label encoder; they cannot be
recognised as separate output symbols unless the alphabet and output layer are extended.
The page-based writer dataset holds out an IAM page per writer, while the personal data use a
fixed-seed 15% line split. A page split and a line split provide different separation: a held-out
personal line can share camera conditions, page background and writing session with training

3.6.1 Choice of reproduction representation
The available writer samples are a few labelled pages photographed after writing. A glyph library
can reuse these observations without fitting a separate generative network for each writer. Haines
et al. provide a relevant precedent: their standard experiments used approximately four A4 pages
per writer [23, p. 4]. This is evidence that a small-page collection can support a glyph-based
approach, not a guarantee that four pages cover every character or writing context. The present
library likewise depends on the characters and variants actually observed.
The offline data also influenced the choice. Graves’s recurrent synthesis model learns from
ordered pen positions and end-of-stroke markers [3, p. 18]; these are not recorded in this project’s
photographs. A skeleton supplies geometric paths, but cannot recover their true order, speed or
pressure. Using it as measured online training data would introduce an unsupported assumption.
Learned image generators remain a valid alternative, including methods that need very few
references from a new writer. For example, One-DM uses one style reference at generation,
but its IAM experiment trains with 339 writers and its implementation uses four RTX 3090
GPUs [22, p. 9]. A small reference set must therefore be distinguished from the corpus and
computation needed to train the generator. Developing and training an additional generator from
first principles on the ODROID was outside the chosen computational scope; no training-time
comparison was measured. The glyph approach instead retains explicit trajectories that can be
scaled, joined and converted directly to machine coordinates. Its trade-off is weaker modelling
of connected, context-dependent letterforms, examined at the end of this subsection.

3.6.3 Skeleton tracing and pen path construction
Ink is an area, whereas the gantry needs a path. Zhang–Suen thinning reduces the line mask
to a one-pixel centreline [26, p. 237]. The complete line is thinned before character cells are
separated. Thinning each cut cell independently would introduce an artificial image edge at every
character boundary and could change the recovered centreline there.
The skeleton is treated as a graph: adjacent ink pixels form edges, and endpoints and junctions
determine where paths begin or split. Redundant diagonal edges are suppressed when an
orthogonal route already connects the same local pixels. The default tracer first splits paths at
junctions, prunes short dangling spurs, then greedily rejoins nearby ends with a continuation
angle below 75○. Thus the default is not simply one pen lift per junction. A separate through-
junction tracer exists but is not enabled by default. Neither procedure establishes natural pen
order: the image records final ink, and several stroke sequences can create the same graph.
Traced paths are divided at the character boundaries and simplified while limiting geometric
deviation, following the polyline-simplification principle of Douglas and Peucker [25]. Short
spurs and cut ligature tails require care because a genuine small stroke can resemble a skeleton

short paths rather than silently discarded.

the corresponding letter instead of splitting it at a vertical cut. Such an overlap is not recorded as
an in-word pen connection. Without this distinction, a crossbar would both contaminate the next
glyph and inflate the profile’s connectedness.

3.6.4 Glyph selection and library quality
The library must retain variation without giving every cut equal credibility. A recogniser can
assign the correct transcript while placing one boundary inside a letter, so alignment is followed
by geometric and consistency checks. Low-confidence lines are filtered relative to the writer’s
own alignment distribution. Per-glyph checks reject implausible width, height, stroke count, pen
length and disagreement between ink width and the allocated character advance.
The width gate allows up to 2.1 x-heights for ordinary characters and 2.9 for wide characters
such as m and w. Further width and height checks use the character’s own distribution, rather than
applying one absolute size to every letter. The 40th-percentile width provides a reference less
easily enlarged by a few cuts that contain part of a neighbour. Tightening is retained only when
enough variants survive to form a usable pool. These gates encode plausible glyph geometry;
their exact values are engineering heuristics, not universal typographic laws.
A cross-writer shape prior supplies a second check. Each variant is represented by a smoothed,
normalised 12×12occupancy descriptor. Pooling descriptors of the same character across writers
gives a robust reference shape. Its role is to reject malformed cuts from a writer’s own library,
not to copy another writer’s letter into it. Internal consensus then favours variants close to the
writer’s typical form. The ordinary variant pool is limited to twelve per character; an accepted
denoised prototype can be prepended as an additional candidate.

Letter shapes alone do not determine the appearance of a line. The profile also stores slant,

3.6.9 Reproduction verification and design limitations
The reproduction checks follow the representations established above: aligned source ink, stored
glyph paths, assembled trajectories and emitted machine commands. Re-rendering the emitted
G-code can reveal a coordinate inversion, scale change, missing stroke or additional joining
segment before drawing. Comparing that render with the selected trajectory checks conversion;
comparing the physical drawing with the render checks execution. These comparisons localise
errors without substituting for end-to-end qualification.
Appendix B shows the same sentence generated from four stored profiles alongside paths
independently parsed from their emitted G-code. Nine controlled conversion fixtures addition-
ally preserved path vertex counts with no clipping and a maximum coordinate discrepancy of
0.0004 mm, consistent with writing coordinates to three decimal places. Joins and ring closure
were disabled only for those correspondence fixtures so that the vertices could be paired; the
four profile illustrations used the normal configuration. Neither check measures the mechanism’s
positional accuracy.
The representation’s main advantage is that its geometry is explicit and inspectable. A defective
glyph can be traced to a source crop, stored paths can be viewed independently of the machine,
and physical size follows from a defined transformation. The main limitation is the loss of
context when a connected word is divided into reusable characters. Real letterforms change
with their neighbours, whereas a finite glyph pool and join rules approximate that dependence.
Skeletons also omit pressure and true temporal order. These losses explain why a plausible letter
library need not reproduce every connected style equally well.
Possible extensions include context-dependent multi-character units, better alignment, trajectory
acquisition and a verified alternative junction tracer. Each addresses a different loss: multi-
character units preserve local letter interactions, trajectory acquisition restores temporal in-
formation, and improved tracing changes how an existing skeleton becomes strokes. Their
implementation would require corresponding changes to extraction, storage or the source data,
rather than a larger candidate search alone. The retained design therefore prioritises extraction
from the available offline pages and explicit control of the plotted geometry. Recovering natural

generator would add a separate training and validation problem. The alternative tracer remains

4.2.1 Test material, configuration and measurement definitions
The final evaluation uses six IAM writers (150, 152, 153, 384, 552 and 588) and five photographed
contributors, denoted A, B, O, T and Y . These are the eleven writers introduced in Section 3.5.
The robot drew fourteen pages because some writers’ text occupies two pages. The pages contain
a common paragraph, a pangram and, where available, text from the writer’s source material.
The analysis contains 184 lines with corresponding transcripts; the writer-model comparison
additionally evaluates one unpaired crop and therefore contains 185 crops.
The physical pages were produced by the gantry under ODROID control. Accuracy was sub-
sequently scored from saved images on the development host. The embedded timing measure-
ments were extracted from the preserved ODROID server log. This arrangement allows large
image sets to be analysed while retaining actual on-device evidence for the time specifications.
Numerical equivalence across processors is not assumed to be a new hardware accuracy test.
The reproduction read-back uses the joint reader used in the Design examples. The separate
reader evaluation compares that reader with the evaluated scratch reader; the selected joint
reader supplies the 94.06% system result and the scratch reader supplies a 94.39% comparison

stroke-normalised classifier at the selected width of 0.17. Appendix C identifies the files by their
content hashes, so checkpoints with the same filename cannot be confused.

5. Discussion
5.1 Critical evaluation of the design
The project connects photographed handwriting, numerical recognition, persistent writer profiles
and a working Cartesian gantry. The results support this central function across eleven writers,
with quality depending on the handwriting and embedded classification time remaining the
principal performance constraint. This evaluation relates those outcomes to the decisions
explained in Design.
5.1.1 Interpretation of results
Useful reproduction is demonstrated, with a clear operating range.The 84.41% read-back
character score is close to the 85% target, but its engineering meaning becomes clearer when the
eleven writers are separated. Seven exceed 85% and four exceed 93%; those four include both
personal contributors and a database writer. Thus the method is capable of substantially better
than the pooled target on demonstrated styles. The lower scores on other writers show that the
remaining improvement concerns consistency across styles, rather than an inability of the gantry
to produce readable text at all. Figure 19 grounds this distinction in physical output.
The association between components per character and legibility is consistent with the limitations
of the chosen representation. A glyph library stores observed character shapes and assembles
them into new contexts. In more joined handwriting, the appearance of a character can depend
strongly on its neighbours, so a cut glyph and a generic connection need not reproduce the same
local shape. The observed rank association of 0.845 supports investigating this mechanism, but
eleven writers do not isolate joining from glyph coverage, input quality or reader preference.
The corresponding identification association is close to zero; it shows that a writer can remain
identifiable even where individual characters are difficult for the reader.
The 86.41% line-identification score and correct attribution of all fourteen page pools are useful
evidence that writer-specific structure survives the full physical process. They answer a different
question from stylistic correlation. The new physical geometric study gives seven-feature shape
correlation 0.697 and nine-feature correlation 0.713, both below 0.85. It also identifies useful
retained structure: slant tracks at 0.991, while ascender extent and character width track at
approximately 0.817 and 0.798. Word spacing, at 0.486, is a weaker channel. These values
motivate improving layout and context while preserving the successful orientation and glyph
information; they do not establish that layout is the sole source of loss.
The writer model also participates in candidate selection, so it cannot be treated as an independent
aesthetic judge of that selection. The geometric study uses no neural classifier, although it still
depends on its descriptor definitions and the different original and reproduced texts. Its separately
standardised coefficient primarily describes cross-writer ordering, and the lower 0.611 absolute
shape variant shows that agreement of feature magnitudes is more demanding. Automated
read-back is likewise an instrument-dependent measure of legibility. It provides a repeatable
comparison, while a blinded human reading and style assessment would test the human-facing

interpretation of the proposal more directly. Neither the physical overlay nor a human rating is
inferred from model confidence.
The classifier results support the chosen architecture.The selected joint reader achieves
94.06% pooled character accuracy, 0.94 percentage points below the 95% target; the NumPy-
trained scratch reader achieves 94.39% on the same evaluation. The scratch reader’s public
and photographed subsets score 94.39% and 94.42%, while the joint reader scores 94.12% and

segmentation is harmless: different text, writers and image conditions may compensate in the

The 98.08% writer-identification point estimate and correct pooling of all eleven writer groups
are particularly strong outcomes for the multi-writer objective. The selected 0.17 stroke width
makes this result relevant to a pen plotter that cannot reproduce each contributor’s original
ink thickness. The three errors also define the limits of the confidence output: one incorrect
classification has an 83.8% posterior, so confidence can guide review but cannot guarantee an
identity. The evaluation is closed set, and its photographed split shares acquisition conditions
with the training material; a new session with the same writers would be a stronger deployment
check.
The selected library reader is retained as the intended demonstration configuration, while the
alternative reader result is reported alongside it. This is a configuration decision rather than
evidence that the library route necessarily gives the higher text score. On these data the scratch
reader is slightly higher; for writer identification the library-trained model is higher. Keeping
both comparisons visible gives a more useful assessment of the implementation than a single
claim that one training route is uniformly superior.
The physical timing result is a practical success.All 25 explicitly completed positive-
character ODROID records satisfy 4 s per character, including generation and drawing. The worst
request still has 5.39 s of allowance remaining. This demonstrates usable physical throughput
across the recorded 43–378-character range, rather than relying on the G-code generator’s
nominal feed calculation. That distinction is important because pen transitions, path length,
GPIO scheduling and generation time all contribute to the request.
The shortest block has the smallest worst-case margin. Generation work is incurred before
the drawing begins, while the allowance grows directly with character count. The measured
worst short job includes 26.5 s of synthesis and approximately 3.248 s per character of drawing.
Treating those two terms as fixed overhead plus a constant drawing rate would put a break-even
length near 35 characters, but this is only a local projection. Search effort, repair and geometry
change with the text, so the result motivates a direct short-request test rather than a claim that all
shorter requests fail. Coordinate clamping in some recorded jobs also prevents the timing pass
being used as a substitute for the separate fidelity result.
The classification-time gap is measurable and actionable.The two ODROID classification
requests exceed their allowances by 31.76 s and 28.94 s. Approximately two thirds of the
combined time is spent in the networks, with the remaining third in image processing. This
makes network execution the largest observed optimisation opportunity, while also showing that
segmentation alone cannot explain the total overrun. A two-request profile identifies the large
contributors; it does not establish a constant cost law for arbitrary pages.

The development-host experiment supplies a concrete alternative: equivalent library networks

Mechanical accuracy is promising, with a precise distinction in what was measured.Every
measured square and triangle feature is within 0.5 mm of nominal, and the square’s mean absolute
side error is 0.175 mm. These are useful results for the calibrated belt-and-rail mechanism.
The triangle’s unequal sloped sides additionally suggest an alignment effect that a side-length-
only square check cannot expose. The apparent 0.706○shear diagnostic offers a direction for
adjustment, conditional on the assumed height and other geometric errors.
Neither equal side lengths nor small side-length errors fix the figure’s absolute placement on
the page. A translated figure can retain the same lengths, and repeated drawings can vary
even when one specimen is accurate. The dimensional result should therefore be credited for
what it demonstrates: a physically realised coordinate-to-motion path with sub-0.5 mm length
differences on the measured features. A repeated-word overlay and a referenced bed-coordinate
grid would extend that evidence to the proposal’s positional and repetition objectives.
Relationship to published approaches.The sequence reader follows the CNN–BiLSTM–
CTC architecture discussed by Kızılırmak and Yanıko˘glu [12, pp. 4–5]. The project’s measured
CER is 5.94% for the selected reader and 5.61% for the scratch reader, obtained directly from
Equation 33. This is evidence of a functioning implementation in the same task family. It is not
a controlled ranking against a published test result: the distributor’s validation partition, training
mixture, checkpoint selection and decoding protocol are not a matched published test protocol.
In particular, the positional word-match score here is not directly comparable with published
WER.
The reproduction strategy has a closer practical relationship to example-based approaches. Haines
et al. demonstrate synthesis using a small collection of handwritten pages and explicit extracted
forms [23, p. 4]. That supports the use of a small personal dataset as a design rationale without
establishing an identical output quality. Graves’s sequence generator uses ordered pen-coordinate
observations [3, p. 18]; this project starts with offline photographs and does not observe those
temporal paths. Modern image generators can model richer context, including One-DM’s use
of a single reference at generation time, but that reference count must be distinguished from
the corpus and hardware used to train the generator [22, p. 9]. These comparisons explain the
selected scope: an inspectable geometric system built from the available data, with context
modelling as its principal reproduction trade-off.

The glyph library fits the data and exposes its own limitations.An explicit library permits
size, slant, spacing and machine coordinates to be examined directly. It also allows the same
stored writer to be used for different text without fitting a new generator for each request. These
advantages are borne out by eleven functioning profiles and the different physical outputs. The
price is a finite set of observed shapes, with limited information about how a writer changes
a letter between neighbours. Multiple-character units or context-conditioned variants would
address that loss more directly than increasing candidate count alone. They would also need
reliable extraction and enough examples of the relevant combinations.
The first-principles and embedded-processing commitments of the proposal also constrain the
interpretation of a library speed remedy. A framework benchmark is a useful engineering
comparison; it does not by itself establish compliance with the original library restriction. A
batched numerical implementation could retain that contribution, while compiled kernels for
the identified hot loops offer a second route. Neither has an embedded compliance result in the
present measurements. The practical workflow also separates off-device training from embedded
operation. The supplied training records substantiate the numerical implementation and the cost
of model preparation, while the proposal’s intended embedded training mode is not demonstrated
by those off-device runs. This distinction keeps the substantial implementation contribution and
the final operating arrangement clear.
Economic feasibility.The author’s current build-cost estimate is approximately R4 000. The
ODROID was borrowed at no purchase cost and will be returned after the project. This reduces
the prototype’s cash expenditure, but a replacement board would be part of a reproducible
deployment cost. The ledger and subsequent purchases require reconciliation before a precise
total is claimed; the later listed clipboard, paper, pen and estimated training electricity amount to

Using the available board allowed the project to prioritise the complete mechanical and image-

ments do not show what a different board would have achieved. The 2.76-fold development-host

assertion that a more expensive platform would necessarily meet R4. A future cost compar-

acceleration route.
5.1.3 Unsolved problems
The main reproduction problem is the variation between writers. Improvements should target the
poorer cases without losing the high-performing printed and lightly joined styles. A useful next
experiment would keep the reader, writer model and input text fixed while comparing current
single-glyph paths with selected multi-character units. That would test the proposed context
explanation directly. The new geometric study establishes a physical-output baseline; matching
original and reproduced sentences and repeating capture sessions would better separate writer
fidelity from content and acquisition effects.
The timing problem requires equivalent-output optimisation. The reduced-resolution experiment
is valuable precisely because it measures the cost: it returns fewer lines on every comparable
page and fails to segment two pages. It therefore cannot be accepted simply because its projected

measure the ODROID’s hot loops and memory use, then compare a batched numerical route or

The remaining mechanical work is a stronger positional qualification rather than an unsupported

5.1.4 Strong points of the design
The functioning chain is a principal strength: the system captures pages, recognises text, iden-
tifies writers, stores trajectories and produces real handwriting. Eleven styles are exercised in
recognition and physical reproduction, seven exceed the reproduction character target, and all
eleven writer-validation groups are identified correctly when pooled. These results substantiate
the multi-writer objective.
An output can be followed through its source crop, normalised glyph, placement and emitted
command. The independent G-code checks and physical measurements support this inspectable
separation, allowing an improvement to be tested at a known interface while retaining the
successful stages.
Operation-level checks and trained results support the numerical implementation: the numerical
writer reaches 95.51% on the common evaluation, and the numerical reader is competitive with
the joint reader. The framework route supplies a practical reference and the stronger selected
writer model. Together they support the implementation contribution and the final model choice.
5.1.5 Expected failure conditions
Strong cursive connections, scarce character variants and text outside the available alphabet are
the most relevant reproduction limits. A profile can preserve only the geometry it contains or the
approximations selected for missing characters. Excessive layout extent can also cause clipping
or coordinate clamping. A request should therefore be inspected in the preview and validated
against the writing area before physical execution; a completed interpreter run alone is not proof
of an unchanged trajectory.
For reading, unusually sparse pages, touching lines, page curvature and strong perspective
changes can defeat the geometric preparation even when a cropped-line network is accurate.
The Design’s real capture examples illustrate these conditions. The writer classifier always
chooses among its eleven represented identities, so an unfamiliar writer can receive a confident

sample does not establish a universally safe rejection threshold. Page pooling improves the

The gantry uses open-loop step counts. A missed step changes the physical position without

prototype whose demonstrated successes can be repeated with an identified configuration, and
whose extensions have specific measurements to target.
5.2 Considerations in the design
5.2.1 Ergonomics
The browser interface places text entry, captured-page review, writer selection and output preview
on the operator’s normal screen. Calibration and a writing-area indication reduce setup ambiguity,
while the preview permits inspection before movement. Network operation is the selected
arrangement; the ODROID also has a display interface [34].
The page and pen still require manual positioning, and the motion area must remain accessible
for loading while being kept clear during execution. A more complete user flow would confirm
the paper restraint, valid calibration, selected profile and preview bounds before a job, and
clearly distinguish generation, running, stopped and completed states. These are consequences
of combining a software interface with a moving mechanism, rather than cosmetic interface
additions.
5.2.2 Health and safety
The moving carriage, pulleys and belts present pinch points, and a protruding pen or tool can
contact a hand in the working area. The installed end stops, limits, abort handling and controlled
setup procedure reduce exposure during normal laboratory operation. The software stop is
useful operational control; independent removal of motor power would provide protection if
the controller or network became unresponsive. The driver and transistor temperature, wiring
restraint and supply enclosure also require attention because the motor circuits carry the actual
load currents.
The 24 V motor rail and 3.3 V logic are separate from the mains inputs of the purchased supplies.
Low internal voltage does not establish mains insulation or enclosure quality. The supply labels
and installed circuit should therefore be part of the final hardware record. A flyback path for

5.2.3 Environmental impact
The borrowed ODROID will be returned after the project. Reusable motors, rails, drivers and
replaceable printed parts allow components to be repaired or used in other work. The printed-part
material is not identified in the retained record, so no material-specific recycling claim is made.
Paper, pens and training electricity are the main recurring consumables identified by the author.
Previewing jobs and testing geometry digitally can reduce wasted paper, while real physical tests
remain necessary to establish performance. The reported R500 training-electricity allowance is a
cost estimate; no total energy or environmental saving is calculated from it without power and
runtime measurements. Electronics, plastics and remaining consumables should be separated
and handled through appropriate local collection routes at end of life.
5.2.4 Social and legal impact
The intended benefit is personalised writing from digital text, including possible use by someone
who cannot comfortably produce their own handwriting. The present system demonstrates
the technical mechanism; accessibility or clinical benefit has not been evaluated with users.
Reproducing another person’s style also creates a risk of impersonation. Samples and profiles
should be used with permission, and generated material should be identified as machine-produced
where its origin matters.
The author confirms that all five personal contributors signed consent forms. Those signatures
should be retained securely as supporting records; publishing them in the report would un-
necessarily expose identifying material. Identifiable handwriting and writer profiles require
consideration under South Africa’s Protection of Personal Information Act, including minimal
collection, a lawful processing basis, retention limits and security safeguards [40, pp. 8–9, 11].
Consent is one lawful basis and does not itself establish every aspect of compliance. The applic-
able database terms must also accompany use of the IAM material. The local-network server
and stored profiles should have access restricted to the intended operator; the prototype is not
presented as a publicly secured handwriting service.
5.2.5 Ethics clearance
Signed consent was obtained from the five personal contributors. No institutional clearance or
exemption reference is included in the retained records; participant consent is not presented as
evidence of institutional approval.

A.3 Morphology, thinning and path simplification
For binary mask M and neighbourhood K, dilation is the maximum of translated mask values
and erosion is their minimum. Closing applies dilation followed by erosion with the same
footprint [35, pp. 533, 541]. For a binary mask and symmetric flat footprint, the set operations
correspond to neighbourhood maximum and minimum. The implementation obtains rectangular
neighbourhood counts through prefix sums; binary comparisons then implement these operations.

Connectivity analysis uses horizontal runs and union operations with diagonal overlap admitted,
rather than a library component-labelling call.
The thinning conditions follow Zhang and Suen [ 26, p. 237]. The method inspects the eight
neighbours P2, . . . ,P9 clockwise from north. A removable foreground pixel has two to six
foreground neighbours and exactly one background-to-foreground transition around that ring.
The two alternating subpasses additionally require, respectively,
P2P4P6=0,P 4P6P8=0 (first subpass),
P2P4P8=0,P 2P6P8=0 (second subpass). (40)
During thinning of the actual Owen crop, the first recorded deletion in subpass one was pixel
(y,x)=(12,190) with neighbours (P2, . . . ,P9)=(0,0,1,1,1,1,0,0) . Its neighbour count is four
and its circular transition count is one. Both first-subpass products are zero because P2=0 and
P8=0 , so the pixel is deleted when the subpass mask is applied. The deletion is applied with the
other eligible pixels in that subpass.
Candidates in Equation 40 are deleted together in each subpass, and the pair of subpasses repeats
until unchanged. This reduces thickness while limiting topology changes; it cannot restore ink
already lost during thresholding. The same thinning primitive supports stroke normalisation,
glyph tracing and the diagnostic retracing route.
The default tracer begins at graph nodes whose degree differs from two, follows unused pixel
edges, and then recovers remaining closed loops. Short dangling spurs are pruned, and nearby
chain ends are greedily rejoined when their continuation angle is below 75○. This remains
a geometric estimate rather than a verified natural handwriting order. Polyline simplification
compares interior points with the line through the endpoints, retaining a point and splitting
recursively when the deviation exceeds tolerance. The code adapts the polyline reduction
method [25]. Near-closed paths are first split at a distant point so that a zero-length endpoint chord
does not collapse the loop. The extraction tolerance is 0.6 source pixels before normalisation;
final path smoothing and machine decimation occur in their own coordinate units.
